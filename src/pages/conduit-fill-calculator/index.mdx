A raceway can't be packed full. The NEC limits how much of the inside of a conduit the conductors may occupy, so that heat can escape and the wires can be pulled in without damage. This calculator adds up the conductor areas and finds the smallest trade size that stays within the limit for the raceway you choose: EMT, rigid metal conduit (RMC) or rigid PVC Schedule 40. It handles any mix of conductor sizes.

## How it works

<div class="formula">Total area = Σ (conductor area × quantity)<br/>Allowed area = conduit internal area × fill limit<br/>Fill limit: 1 conductor 53 %, 2 conductors 31 %, over 2 conductors 40 %</div>

The conductor areas come from Chapter 9, Table 5. The fill limits come from Table 1, and the conduit areas come from Table 4, which lists them separately for each raceway type under its own article. Every conductor counts, including the equipment grounding and bonding conductors, which Chapter 9 requires you to include in the calculation.

## Worked examples

**Nine 12 AWG THHN conductors.** 9 × 0.0133 = 0.1197 in². The 40 % area of ½ inch EMT is 0.122 in², so it fits. Ten conductors come to 0.133 in², which is too much, and the answer moves up to ¾ inch.

**More 12 AWG.** ¾ inch EMT (0.213 in² at 40 %) holds sixteen (0.2128 in²), and 1 inch EMT (0.346 in²) holds twenty-six (0.3458 in²). These are the same counts as the same-size tables in the code's Annex C, which you can use to check the calculator.

**A mixed set.** Four 10 AWG, three 12 AWG and one 8 AWG: 0.0844 + 0.0399 + 0.0366 = 0.1609 in². That is more than the ½ inch limit of 0.122 in² and less than the ¾ inch limit of 0.213 in², so the answer is ¾ inch EMT.

**Two conductors.** Two 4 AWG come to 0.1648 in². With two conductors the limit is 31 %, which is 0.165 in² for ¾ inch EMT, so it fits by a very small margin.

**One conductor.** A single 4/0 AWG (0.3237 in²) is held to 53 %. ¾ inch EMT allows 0.283 in², which is too little, and 1 inch allows 0.458 in², so the answer is 1 inch.

## Why the percentages differ

A single conductor can fill more of the tube because it can't jam against others. Two conductors can wedge and are held to the lowest figure, 31 %, because their geometry makes them the most likely to bind during a pull. Three or more conductors are held to 40 %.

## What the calculator assumes

- **THHN, THWN and THWN-2 dimensions.** Other insulation types, such as XHHW, have different areas. Use Table 5 for your conductor type.
- **Three raceway types.** EMT, RMC and PVC Schedule 40 are included. PVC Schedule 80, flexible conduit, liquidtight and other raceways have different internal areas and are not. The same conductors can need a different trade size in each, because PVC has thicker walls than EMT and so less room inside.
- **Table values for RMC and PVC are derived.** The total internal areas are entered, and the 53, 31 and 40 percent columns are calculated from them and rounded to three decimals. The printed code table can differ in the last digit.
- **Round, stranded conductors**, as in Table 5.
- **Full-length raceway.** Short nipples of 24 inches or less are allowed a higher fill under a note to Chapter 9. The calculator doesn't apply it.

## What fill doesn't cover

Fill is not the only limit on a conduit run. Conductors in a raceway also need the ampacity adjustment for more than three current-carrying conductors, which the <a href="/wire-size-calculator/">wire size calculator</a> applies. Long runs and many bends make a pull harder even when the fill passes, and the code limits the total bends between pull points. Pull-box and junction-box sizing follow their own rules.

## Common mistakes

- **Leaving out the ground wire.**
- **Using Table 5 areas for a different insulation type.**
- **Treating the 40 % as a target.** It is a ceiling, and many installers go a size up for easier pulls.
- **Mixing up trade size and inside diameter.** Trade sizes are nominal.

## Questions

### How many 12 AWG wires fit in ½ inch conduit?
Nine THHN conductors in EMT or RMC, and eight in PVC Schedule 40. One more is over the 40 % limit.

### Is the grounding conductor included?
Yes. Add it as one more conductor of its own size.

### What is the fill for three conductors?
Over two conductors, the limit is 40 %, whatever the conduit type.

### Can I use this for PVC?
Yes, for Schedule 40. Choose it in the raceway list. Eight 12 AWG fit in ½ inch PVC-40, where nine fit in EMT or RMC.

## Worked example: residential branch circuit panel run

A residential panel requires the following conductors in a single EMT conduit:
- 3 × 12 AWG THHN (20A circuit 1)
- 3 × 12 AWG THHN (20A circuit 2)
- 3 × 14 AWG THHN (15A lighting circuit)
- 1 × 12 AWG THHN (EGC)

Total: 9 × 12 AWG (0.0133 in² each) + 3 × 14 AWG (0.0097 in² each) + 1 × 12 AWG EGC.

**Area calculation:**
- 10 × 12 AWG: 10 × 0.0133 = 0.133 in²
- 3 × 14 AWG: 3 × 0.0097 = 0.0291 in²
- Total: 0.1621 in²

**Conduit selection (40% fill, 11+ conductors):**
- ¾ in EMT: 0.213 in² at 40% → 0.1621 fits ✓
- Check Annex C Table C.1: ¾ in EMT holds 16 × 12 AWG, but we have a mix.

**Minimum trade size: ¾ inch EMT.**

Note: with 11 current-carrying conductors (three 3-wire circuits = 9 conductors + EGC is not counted as current-carrying), the ampacity of 12 AWG must be derated by the Table 310.15(B)(3)(a) bundling factor. With 7–9 conductors, multiply by 0.70. This reduces the 90°C ampacity of 12 AWG THHN from 30 A to 21 A — still above the 20 A breaker, so the fill is acceptable. At 10–20 conductors use 0.50, reducing to 15 A, which is below 20 A. At that point use 10 AWG THHN or separate the circuits into two conduits.

## Ampacity derating table (NEC 310.15(B)(3)(a))

| Number of current-carrying conductors | Ampacity adjustment |
|---|---|
| 4–6 | 80 % |
| 7–9 | 70 % |
| 10–20 | 50 % |
| 21–30 | 45 % |
| 31–40 | 40 % |
| 41+ | 35 % |

Fill calculation and ampacity derating are two separate checks. A conduit can pass the fill limit and still require larger conductors because the bundled ampacity falls below the breaker rating.

## Raceway comparison: EMT vs RMC vs PVC-40

| Raceway | Internal area ½ in | 12 AWG capacity | Weight | Conduit bends |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| EMT | 0.304 in² | 9 | Lightest | Use conduit bender |
| RMC | 0.314 in² | 9 | Heaviest | Threaded fittings |
| PVC-40 | 0.285 in² | 8 | Light | Heat bending |

PVC-40 has thicker walls than EMT, so its internal area is smaller and it holds one fewer conductor in some trade sizes. PVC-40 is listed for direct burial and wet locations; EMT is not suitable for direct burial or areas where severe physical damage is likely.

## More Q&As

### Does the neutral conductor count toward fill?
Yes. Every conductor in the conduit counts for fill calculation, including the neutral and any EGC. Only the current-carrying conductor count affects the ampacity derating factor. A grounded neutral is counted as current-carrying unless it carries only the unbalanced current of a 3-wire or 4-wire circuit (NEC 310.15(E)).

### Can I run two conduits side by side to split a large fill?
Yes. Two conduits side by side is a common solution when a single conduit would exceed the fill limit or require heavy ampacity derating. Each conduit gets roughly half the conductors and both conduits pass the fill check.

### What is a short nipple, and why does it allow more fill?
A conduit nipple 24 inches or less can be filled to 60% (NEC Chapter 9, Note 4), compared to 40% for a full run. This applies to the short section connecting two enclosures, not to a full run through walls. A ½ inch EMT nipple at 60% holds about 15 × 14 AWG instead of 12.
