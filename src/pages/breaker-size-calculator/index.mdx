This calculator finds the minimum overcurrent device rating for a load under the NEC continuous-load rule, then rounds up to the next standard size. It does not select the conductor, and it does not replace the code or the authority having jurisdiction.

## The rule

A continuous load is one expected to run for three hours or more. For these loads, NEC 210.20(A) for branch circuits and 215.3 for feeders require the overcurrent device to be rated at no less than 125% of the continuous load plus 100% of the noncontinuous load. Standard ratings are listed in 240.6(A).

<div class="formula">Minimum rating = 1.25 × I<sub>continuous</sub> + I<sub>noncontinuous</sub><br/>Breaker = next standard rating ≥ minimum</div>

Standard ratings start at 10 A for circuit breakers in the 2023 edition (the 2020 list starts at 15 A), then run 15, 20, 25, 30, 35, 40, 45, 50, 60, 70, 80, 90 and 100 A, continuing at 110, 125, 150, 175, 200, 225, 250, 300, 350, 400 A and larger. Fuses also come in 1, 3, 6 and 601 A. If you work to the 2020 code, ignore the 10 A rating. Check the list against your edition before you rely on it.

## Worked example

A 32 A EV charger is a continuous load. 1.25 × 32 = 40 A, which is a standard rating, so a 40 A breaker is the minimum. For a 40 A charger, 1.25 × 40 = 50 A, so a 50 A breaker.

A mixed case: 16 A of continuous load plus 10 A of noncontinuous load gives 1.25 × 16 + 10 = 30 A, so a 30 A breaker.

A load of 25 A continuous gives 31.25 A, and the next standard size above that is 35 A.

## What this leaves out

- **Conductor sizing.** The conductor must also carry the 125% current after any ambient and bundling adjustments, using the 310.16 tables and the termination temperature limits in 110.14(C). Use the breaker as the starting point and check the cable separately.
- **Motors and other special loads.** Motor circuits follow Article 430, with their own rules for the protective device. Air conditioners and heat pumps follow Article 440.
- **100%-rated assemblies.** Some listed breaker and enclosure combinations are rated to carry 100% of continuous load. The calculator doesn't model them.
- **EV equipment.** Article 625 gives rules for EV supply equipment, and the nameplate rating of the equipment governs.

## Worked workflow: an EV charger circuit

Suppose a charger is rated 40 A. Electric vehicle supply equipment is treated as a continuous load, so the breaker is sized at 1.25 × 40 = 50 A, which is a standard rating. The conductor then has to be at least adequate for 50 A under the code's tables and the terminal temperature rating of the equipment, and the circuit is protected at 50 A. A 50 A breaker supports 40 A continuous, and that is 80% of its rating.

## Branch circuits and feeders

The same 125% logic runs through a feeder. A feeder serving a mix of continuous and noncontinuous loads sums the noncontinuous load at 100% and the continuous load at 125%, then applies any demand factors the code permits to the loads. This calculator takes two numbers, so for a feeder, work out the total continuous and noncontinuous currents first, then enter them.

## When the 125% factor does not apply

If every load on the circuit is noncontinuous, the minimum is 100% of the load. Enter zero for the continuous load and the calculator will not derate it. Some assemblies are listed for operation at 100% of rating, in which case the rule is relaxed. Those need manufacturer documentation and are outside the calculator.

## Documenting the result

The print button produces a page listing the inputs and result. For a submittal, add the code edition and the breaker's interrupting rating, which must be at least the available fault current at the panel.

## Questions

### Why 125%?
A load that runs for hours heats the breaker and its terminals more than a short peak does. Sizing at 125% keeps the continuous current at 80% of the device rating.

### Can I round down to the lower breaker size?
No. For this calculation the device must be at least the calculated minimum, so a 31.25 A result needs 35 A, not 30 A. The next-size-up allowance in 240.4(B) is a separate rule about protecting conductors and doesn't change this.

### Is the 125% rule the same outside the US?
No. IEC systems size the protective device against design current and the conductor's current-carrying capacity under a different method. This page is NEC-only.

### What counts as continuous?
Loads expected to run three hours or more, such as EV charging, lighting in commercial buildings and some process equipment.

## Sizing a breaker for common loads

Minimum breaker size (NEC 125 % rule) for continuous loads:

| Continuous load (A) | 125 % minimum (A) | Standard breaker size |
|---|---|---|
| 10 A | 12.5 A | 15 A |
| 12 A | 15.0 A | 15 A |
| 16 A | 20.0 A | 20 A |
| 20 A | 25.0 A | 25 A |
| 24 A | 30.0 A | 30 A |
| 32 A | 40.0 A | 40 A |
| 40 A | 50.0 A | 50 A |
| 48 A | 60.0 A | 60 A |
| 56 A | 70.0 A | 70 A |

The 80 % rule and the 125 % rule are the same thing stated from different directions. A continuous load must stay at 80 % or below the breaker's rating, which is equivalent to sizing the breaker at 125 % of the continuous load.

## EV charger breaker sizing

Electric vehicle supply equipment (EVSE) is classified as a continuous load under NEC 625.42. For a Level 2 charger, the nameplate lists the rated output current. Apply 125 %:

| Charger output (A) | Minimum breaker (A) | Conductor (AWG, 75 °C) |
|---|---|---|
| 16 A | 20 A | 12 AWG |
| 24 A | 30 A | 10 AWG |
| 32 A | 40 A | 8 AWG |
| 40 A | 50 A | 8 AWG |
| 48 A | 60 A | 6 AWG |

A 48 A charger is the largest common Level 2 output. The 60 A breaker is correct — do not use 50 A because it cannot carry 48 A continuously at 100 % of its rating.

## Feeder sizing with mixed continuous and noncontinuous loads

For a feeder or subpanel serving several circuits, sum the loads separately:

1. Total noncontinuous load at 100 %.
2. Total continuous load at 125 %.
3. Add both. That sum is the minimum rating for the feeder overcurrent device.

**Example:** a panel serves 20 A of continuous lighting and 30 A of noncontinuous receptacle loads. Minimum feeder breaker: 20 × 1.25 + 30 = 55 A. Next standard size: 60 A. The feeder conductor must be rated at least 55 A — 6 AWG copper at 75 °C covers 65 A, which is adequate.

## From breaker size to conductor

The breaker is not the last step. The conductor must be rated to carry the minimum calculated current (before rounding to a standard breaker size) after any adjustment for ambient temperature or bundling. The key rule is NEC 110.14(C): the conductor must be rated at the terminal's temperature rating, typically 75 °C for circuit breakers above 100 A.

For a 40 A breaker: use 8 AWG copper at 75 °C (50 A ampacity). The 50 A ampacity leaves room for bundling derating. Avoid matching conductor ampacity exactly to the minimum calculated current if there is any chance of bundling. ## More Q&As

### Does the 125 % rule apply to both the breaker and the conductor?
Yes. NEC 210.20(A) requires both the overcurrent device and the conductor to be rated for 125 % of the continuous load. A 20 A breaker with 14 AWG (15 A ampacity) fails the conductor side of this test for a continuous load above 12 A.

### Can I use a 100-rated breaker to skip the 125 % multiplier?
In theory, yes. Some listed assemblies — specific breaker and panelboard combinations — are rated at 100 % of continuous load. The listing must be verified on the equipment nameplate. Standard residential breakers are not 100 %-rated.

### How do I size a breaker for a 240 V, 7,200 W continuous load?
Current: 7,200 / 240 = 30 A. At 125 %: 37.5 A. Next standard size: 40 A. Conductor: 8 AWG at 75 °C covers 50 A — adequate for the 40 A breaker.

### Does the breaker size change at 240 V versus 120 V?
No — the breaker is sized from the current, not the voltage. The same 3,600 W load draws 30 A at 120 V but only 15 A at 240 V, so the 240 V circuit uses a smaller breaker for the same power.
