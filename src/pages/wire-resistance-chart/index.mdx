This chart lists the diameter, cross-sectional area and DC resistance of AWG conductors. Resistance is given for copper and aluminum at 20 °C and at 75 °C, a common conductor rating. The values are calculated for a solid round conductor from the AWG definition and the resistivity of each metal.

## How it is calculated

<div class="formula">d = 0.127 × 92<sup>(36 − n)/39</sup> mm<br/>R = ρ × L / A, with ρ(T) = ρ<sub>20</sub> × (1 + α × (T − 20))</div>

The AWG number n gives the diameter d, and the area is π d² / 4. The resistivity ρ at 20 °C is 0.017241 Ω·mm²/m for copper and 0.028264 for aluminum. The temperature coefficient α is 0.00393 per °C for copper and 0.00403 for aluminum. The <a href="/awg-to-mm2-converter/">AWG to mm² converter</a> gives the diameter and area for any gauge.

## Worked example

A 14 AWG copper cable runs 200 ft to a load, so the loop is 400 ft of conductor. At 20 °C the resistance is 2.525 Ω per 1,000 ft × 0.4 = 1.01 Ω. At 10 A that loses 10.1 V, which is 8.4 % of a 120 V supply. With the conductor at 75 °C the resistance rises to 3.071 × 0.4 = 1.23 Ω and the drop to 12.3 V. Moving to 10 AWG (0.999 Ω per 1,000 ft at 20 °C) cuts the loss to 4.0 V.

## Differences from published tables

- **Stranding.** Stranded conductors are a few percent higher in resistance than solid ones of the same size, because the individual wires follow a slightly longer path. Code and manufacturer tables for stranded wire give the higher figure. Use those for a final design.
- **Coating.** Tinned or coated copper has slightly higher resistance than bare.
- **AC resistance.** At 60 Hz, large conductors have a small skin effect, and reactance adds to the impedance. For long, large conductors use an impedance table.

## Using it

Resistance is the starting point for voltage drop, power loss and fault current. Power lost in a conductor is I² × R, so a 10 A load through 1.01 Ω wastes 101 W as heat. The <a href="/voltage-drop-chart/">voltage drop chart</a> turns these resistances into volts per amp and maximum lengths.

## Questions

### What is the resistance of 12 AWG copper wire?
1.588 Ω per 1,000 ft at 20 °C, or 1.932 Ω at 75 °C, for a solid conductor.

### Why does resistance go up as the AWG number goes down?
A smaller AWG number is a thicker wire with more area, so its resistance is lower. The numbers run backwards: 4/0 is the thickest and 24 the thinnest on this chart.

### How does temperature change the resistance?
Copper rises by about 0.4 % per °C. From 20 °C to 75 °C that is about 22 %.

### How do I find the total resistance of a circuit loop?
Add the resistance of the outgoing conductor and the return conductor. For a 100 ft run with 12 AWG copper, the loop is 200 ft. At 20 °C: 1.588 × 0.2 = 0.318 Ω. Apply Ohm's law to get the voltage drop.

### Is aluminum resistance close enough to substitute for copper?
No. Aluminum has about 1.64 times the resistance of copper for the same AWG. A 2 AWG aluminum cable has roughly the same resistance as a 4 AWG copper cable. Aluminum is used in feeders for cost and weight, but the cable must be sized up accordingly.

## Voltage drop from resistance

Voltage drop is the most direct use of the chart. The formula is:

<div class="formula">V<sub>drop</sub> = I × R<sub>loop</sub></div>

where I is the current in amps and R<sub>loop</sub> is the total resistance of the outgoing and return conductors. For a 50 A load through 150 ft of 6 AWG copper (loop = 300 ft), the resistance at 20 °C is 0.395 × 0.3 = 0.1185 Ω. Voltage drop is 50 × 0.1185 = 5.9 V on a 240 V circuit, or 2.5 %. The NEC recommends not exceeding 3 % on branch circuits (5 % to the final load including feeders). The <a href="/voltage-drop-calculator/">voltage drop calculator</a> handles arbitrary lengths and conductor types in one step.

## Power loss

The same resistance causes heat. Power dissipated in the conductors is:

<div class="formula">P<sub>loss</sub> = I² × R<sub>loop</sub></div>

For the 50 A, 6 AWG example above, that is 50² × 0.1185 = 296 W lost in the cable run. At typical electricity rates, running a 50 A circuit continuously for a year costs roughly 260 kWh just in cable heat. This is why upsizing conductors on long, heavily loaded runs has a real payback.

Aluminum feeders save cost and weight on long runs, but the connection is more critical. Aluminum oxidizes rapidly and connections must use approved anti-oxidant compound with approved connectors. Aluminum is not permitted in sizes below 8 AWG under most code interpretations for branch circuits.

## NEC ampacity and resistance — the two separate limits

A conductor must meet two criteria:

1. **Ampacity** (NEC Table 310.12): the maximum continuous current the insulation can handle at a given ambient temperature.
2. **Voltage drop**: not a code requirement on most circuits, but a performance issue. The NEC only recommends 3 % on branch circuits and 5 % total to the utilization equipment.

A 10 AWG copper conductor in conduit has a 75 °C ampacity of 35 A. It may also carry 50 A momentarily, but its voltage drop will be higher, and its temperature will exceed the rating. Sizing from ampacity alone without checking voltage drop is a common error on long runs.

## Temperature derating

Resistance in this chart is at 20 °C (68 °F), the standard reference. The table also gives 75 °C values, which are closer to actual operating conditions. For intermediate temperatures, the linear formula applies:

<div class="formula">R(T) = R<sub>20</sub> × (1 + 0.00393 × (T − 20)) for copper</div>

For aluminum, substitute α = 0.00403.

Ampacity also derates with ambient temperature. If the air around a conduit is 40 °C rather than the standard 30 °C, NEC Table 310.15(B)(1) applies a correction factor. At 40 °C ambient the factor is 0.91 on 75 °C conductors. A 35 A conductor is derated to 32 A. This is separate from the resistance increase — the derating protects the insulation, not just the resistance calculation.

## Fault current and cable impedance

On short circuits, the resistance of the circuit conductors limits the available fault current. A large transformer may supply 20 kA at its terminals, but 30 m of 4 AWG copper (loop resistance about 0.012 Ω at 75 °C) limits the fault current at the far end to roughly 20,000 / (1 + 0.012 × 20,000 / 480) ≈ 10 kA. This is why protective devices must be rated for the current at their location, not just at the transformer, and why the <a href="/cable-fault-current-calculator/">cable end fault current calculator</a> is a useful check after the transformer chart.
