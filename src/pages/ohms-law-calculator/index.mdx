## Formulas

<div class="formula">V = I × R<br/>P = V × I = I² × R = V² / R</div>

Enter any two of V, I, R, P and the other two follow. All values are for DC or for the RMS values of a purely resistive AC load.

## Worked example

A 6 Ω heating element runs from 12 V. Current is 12 / 6 = 2 A. Power is 12 × 2 = 24 W. Enter 12 V and 6 Ω above to confirm.

## How to use the calculator

Fill in any two of the four boxes and leave the other two empty. The calculator solves for the remaining pair. Values must be positive, and results show to six significant figures.

## All six combinations

Each pair of known values leads to a different pair of formulas:

- **V and I:** R = V / I, P = V × I
- **V and R:** I = V / R, P = V² / R
- **V and P:** I = P / V, R = V² / P
- **I and R:** V = I × R, P = I² × R
- **I and P:** V = P / I, R = P / I²
- **R and P:** V = √(P × R), I = √(P / R)

## More worked examples

A 60 W lamp on a 120 V supply: R = 120² / 60 = 240 Ω and I = 60 / 120 = 0.5 A. That 240 Ω is the hot resistance. Measured with a meter while cold, a tungsten filament reads far lower, which is why incandescent lamps draw a surge when switched on.

A 2,300 W heater on 230 V: I = 2,300 / 230 = 10 A and R = 230² / 2,300 = 23 Ω. Check: 10 A × 23 Ω = 230 V.

## Why current matters more than voltage for heating

The form P = I² × R explains why cables get warm. Heat in a conductor rises with the square of the current, so doubling the current quadruples the loss in the same cable. This is the reason conductor size is chosen from the current and not from the voltage.

## Where Ohm's law stops applying

The law holds for a fixed resistance. It does not describe devices whose resistance changes with voltage or temperature, such as diodes, LEDs, and filament lamps (resistance rises with temperature). For AC circuits with inductors or capacitors, replace R with impedance Z, and note that P = V × I then needs the power factor: P = V × I × PF. The <a href="/amps-to-watts-calculator/">amps to watts calculator</a> handles that case. With RMS values on a purely resistive AC load, the formulas on this page apply directly.

## Common mistakes

- **Unit slips.** Milliamps and kilohms are the usual culprits. Convert to amps and ohms first.
- **Mixing peak and RMS.** Mains is quoted as RMS. A 325 V peak is a 230 V RMS supply.
- **Using the wrong voltage.** In a series circuit the voltage across one resistor is not the supply voltage.

## Questions

### What is the formula triangle for Ohm's law?
It is a memory aid with V on top and I and R below. Cover the quantity you want and the other two show the formula, so V = I × R, I = V / R and R = V / I.

### Can Ohm's law be used for AC?
For resistive loads, yes. For motors, transformers and other reactive loads, use impedance and power factor.

### Does it matter if the current is DC or AC?
Not for a pure resistance. For DC the formulas always hold.

### What is the unit of resistance?
The ohm, written Ω, equal to one volt per amp.

### How do I use Ohm's law to size an LED current-limiting resistor?
An LED needs a fixed forward voltage (typically 1.8–3.3 V depending on color) and a maximum current (typically 20 mA). The resistor drops the remaining voltage. For a red LED (2 V forward voltage, 20 mA) on a 5 V supply: R = (5 − 2) / 0.020 = 150 Ω. Use the next standard size up — 180 Ω — to stay safely under the maximum current.

### How do I find the resistance of a cable?
Use Ohm's law with a measured voltage drop and current: R = V / I. Alternatively, calculate it from the cable data: R = ρ × L / A, where ρ is the resistivity (0.0172 Ω·mm²/m for copper), L the length in metres and A the cross-section in mm². The <a href="/wire-resistance-chart/">wire resistance chart</a> gives the result in ohms per 1,000 ft for every AWG size.

### How do I calculate total resistance in series and parallel?
Series: R_total = R₁ + R₂ + R₃ (resistances add directly). Parallel: 1/R_total = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂ + 1/R₃ (reciprocals add, then take the reciprocal of the sum). For two equal resistors in parallel, R_total = R / 2.

### How is Ohm's law used to size a fuse?
Find the normal operating current with I = P / V, then choose a fuse rated just above that current. The fuse must not carry the load continuously at more than its rating, so a 10 A continuous load needs at least a 12.5 A fuse (125 % of running current). For NEC branch circuit protection, use the breaker-sizing rules rather than the calculated current directly.

## Series and parallel circuits

Ohm's law applies to individual components, but circuits combine components in series and parallel. The key identities:

**Series circuit** — components share the same current; voltages add:
- V_total = V₁ + V₂ + V₃
- R_total = R₁ + R₂ + R₃
- I is the same through every component

**Parallel circuit** — components share the same voltage; currents add:
- I_total = I₁ + I₂ + I₃
- 1/R_total = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂ + 1/R₃
- V is the same across every component

**Worked example — series:** Two resistors, 10 Ω and 15 Ω, in series across 50 V. R_total = 25 Ω, I = 50 / 25 = 2 A. Voltage across the 10 Ω resistor: 2 × 10 = 20 V. Voltage across the 15 Ω: 2 × 15 = 30 V. Check: 20 + 30 = 50 V.

**Worked example — parallel:** The same two resistors in parallel across 50 V. Each has 50 V across it. I₁ = 50 / 10 = 5 A, I₂ = 50 / 15 = 3.33 A. Total current = 8.33 A. Equivalent resistance: R = 50 / 8.33 = 6 Ω. Check with formula: 1/R = 1/10 + 1/15 = 0.1 + 0.067 = 0.167, R = 6 Ω.

## Voltage divider

A voltage divider is two resistors in series used to produce a fraction of the supply voltage. The output voltage is taken from the junction of the two resistors:

<div class="formula">V_out = V_in × R₂ / (R₁ + R₂)</div>

A 10 kΩ and a 15 kΩ resistor in series across 5 V give an output of 5 × 15 / (10 + 15) = 3 V at the mid-point. Voltage dividers are used in sensor interfaces and reference circuits. They assume a high-impedance load — if the output drives a low-resistance load, the loaded output voltage drops because the load parallels R₂.

## LED resistor sizing

The most common practical application of Ohm's law in electronics is selecting a current-limiting resistor for an LED. The formula is:

<div class="formula">R = (V_supply − V_forward) / I_LED</div>

| LED color | Typical V_forward | Resistor at 5 V, 20 mA | Resistor at 12 V, 20 mA |
|---|---|---|---|
| Red | 1.8–2.2 V | 140–160 Ω → use 150 Ω | 490–510 Ω → use 510 Ω |
| Green / yellow | 2.0–2.4 V | 130–150 Ω → use 150 Ω | 480–500 Ω → use 510 Ω |
| Blue / white | 3.0–3.4 V | 80–100 Ω → use 100 Ω | 430–450 Ω → use 470 Ω |

Always use the next standard resistor size above the calculated value to keep the current at or below the maximum. The power dissipated in the resistor is P = I² × R — for a 150 Ω resistor at 20 mA: P = 0.02² × 150 = 0.06 W. A quarter-watt resistor is more than adequate.

## Cable resistance and fault current

Ohm's law links conductor resistance to fault current. When a short circuit occurs, the fault current is limited by the total impedance of the loop, including the cable resistance. A lower cable resistance means a higher fault current, which trips protection faster and more reliably.

For a 240 V single-phase circuit with a loop resistance of 0.5 Ω (outgoing and return conductors combined), the prospective fault current is 240 / 0.5 = 480 A. This is the worst-case value; at the transformer terminals the fault current is higher because the cable resistance adds to it. The <a href="/short-circuit-current-calculator/">short-circuit current calculator</a> and the <a href="/wire-resistance-chart/">wire resistance chart</a> together let you work through this for any cable run.

## Power dissipation and thermal limits

The P = I² × R form of Ohm's law explains why electrical safety is about current, not just voltage. Heat generated in a conductor scales with the square of the current:

| Current multiplier | Power multiplier |
|---|---|
| ×1 (baseline) | ×1 |
| ×1.5 | ×2.25 |
| ×2 | ×4 |
| ×3 | ×9 |
| ×5 (starting surge) | ×25 |

Tripling the current produces nine times the heat in the same resistance. This is why motor starting surges — which can reach 5–7× the running current for a few seconds — produce far more conductor heating per second than steady running, and why thermal-magnetic breakers have a time-delay to ride through starting without tripping.

The inverse of this is that doubling the conductor cross-section halves its resistance and cuts the heat loss by half for the same current — the economic argument for upsizing conductors on long, heavily loaded circuits.
