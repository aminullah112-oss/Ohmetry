The calculator answers one question: how many watts of panels are needed to produce a given amount of energy per day. It gives a planning estimate. A real design also checks the roof, the shading, the inverter and the local rules.

## Formula

<div class="formula">Array (W) = daily energy (Wh) / (peak sun hours × performance ratio)<br/>Panels = array (W) / panel rating (W), rounded up</div>

**Peak sun hours (PSH)** are the number of hours per day at an irradiance of 1,000 W/m² that would deliver the same energy as the real day. A site with 5 kWh/m² of daily sunlight has 5 peak sun hours. Values of 3 to 6 are typical, depending on latitude, climate and season. Look up your site in a tool such as NREL's PVWatts or the Global Solar Atlas and use the figure for the plane of your array, not for flat ground.

**Performance ratio (PR)** bundles every loss between the panel's rated output and the energy you can use: heat, dust, wiring, mismatch, inverter conversion. About 0.75 is a common planning value for a well-built system, and a poor one can be lower.

## Worked example

A household uses 30 kWh per day and has 5 peak sun hours. With a PR of 0.75:

Array = 30,000 / (5 × 0.75) = 8,000 W.

With 400 W panels that is 8,000 / 400 = 20 panels. The calculator rounds up, so a result of 7.8 panels becomes 8, and it reports the expected daily output of the rounded-up array.

## Getting the inputs right

- **Daily energy.** Take a year of electricity bills, divide the total kWh by 365, and decide whether you want to cover all of it or only part. Off-grid and winter-limited systems need the worst month, not the average.
- **Peak sun hours.** An annual average hides the winter shortfall. If your goal is year-round cover, size from the weakest month, or accept that the grid or a generator fills the gap.
- **Panel rating.** The rating is measured under standard test conditions of 1,000 W/m² and 25 °C cell temperature. Real cells run hotter, and silicon loses roughly 0.3 to 0.4 % of its output per °C above 25 °C, which is part of the PR.

## What this does not cover

Roof area is a practical limit: a 400 W panel is typically close to 2 m². Shading from chimneys, trees and neighbouring buildings can cut output far more than the PR assumes, especially when one shaded panel limits a string. Panels also lose a fraction of a percent of their output each year. Grid-connected systems also face export rules and inverter size limits that depend on the utility and the country.

## Common mistakes

- **Multiplying panel watts by 24 hours.** Panels produce at full rating only around midday in clear conditions.
- **Forgetting the PR.** Using the nameplate overstates output by a quarter or more.
- **Using a sunny-month figure for a winter-critical load.**

## Questions

### How many panels do I need for a 2,000 kWh monthly bill?
That is about 66.7 kWh per day. At 5 peak sun hours and a PR of 0.75, the array is 17.8 kW, or 45 panels of 400 W.

### How many kWh does a 5 kW array produce per day?
At 5 peak sun hours and a PR of 0.75, about 18.8 kWh.

## Worked example: UK home with winter sizing

A UK household uses 10 kWh/day. The household wants to cover most of its consumption year-round. Worst-month PSH (December, tilted array at 35°): 1.8 PSH.

**Annual average array:** 10,000 / (4.2 × 0.75) = 3,175 W → 8 × 400 W panels.

**Winter-sized array:** 10,000 / (1.8 × 0.75) = 7,407 W → 19 × 400 W panels.

The winter-sized array overproduces by 2.3× in summer. Practical compromises:
- Fit the 8-panel (3.2 kW) array and accept grid top-up in winter.
- Fit the 19-panel array and export surplus in summer under a feed-in tariff.
- Fit 10–12 panels with a battery (stores afternoon surplus for evening use).

For a grid-tied UK system without export constraints, 10–14 panels is a typical design range that balances cost and self-consumption.

## Panel technology comparison

| Technology | Typical efficiency | Temperature coefficient | Best for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Monocrystalline PERC | 20–23 % | −0.35 %/°C | Space-limited roofs |
| Polycrystalline | 16–18 % | −0.40 %/°C | Cost-sensitive installs |
| Bifacial mono | 21–24 % + rear | −0.35 %/°C | Ground mount, flat roofs |
| HJT (heterojunction) | 22–24 % | −0.26 %/°C | Hot climates |
| Thin-film (CdTe) | 16–19 % | −0.25 %/°C | Utility scale, partial shade |

HJT has the lowest temperature coefficient, so it outperforms silicon PERC in hot conditions. A PERC panel rated 400 W at 25 °C delivers about 384 W at 40 °C cell temperature (a common summer afternoon value), while HJT delivers about 393 W.

## Roof area and shading

A 400 W panel is typically 1.72 m × 1.13 m ≈ 1.95 m². Required roof area ≈ panel count × 2 m² (allowing for spacing). A 20-panel, 8 kW array needs roughly 40 m² of unshaded south-facing roof.

Shading is the dominant loss factor not captured in PR. Even partial shading of one cell in a string without bypass diodes can cut the output of the entire string by 50–80 %. Microinverters or DC optimisers mitigate this — worth specifying on complex roofs or those with morning/afternoon shading.

## More Q&As

### Does panel orientation affect the calculation?
Yes. South-facing panels (in the northern hemisphere) at an angle equal to the site latitude capture maximum annual energy. East-west split roofs produce a flatter daily profile — useful for self-consumption — but 10–15 % less annual energy than a south-facing array. The PSH input should reflect the actual array orientation, not horizontal irradiance.

### What is the difference between STC and NOCT ratings?
Standard Test Conditions (STC): 1,000 W/m², 25 °C cell temperature, AM1.5 spectrum. Normal Operating Cell Temperature (NOCT): 800 W/m², 20 °C ambient, 1 m/s wind. NOCT ratings are typically 10–15 % lower than STC and closer to real-world performance on a sunny afternoon. Performance ratios of 0.75–0.80 implicitly account for the gap.

### How long do solar panels last, and how much do they degrade?
Modern silicon panels are warranted for 25–30 years at 80–87 % of original output. Degradation is typically 0.4–0.7 %/year, with a faster first-year drop (1–3 %) due to light-induced degradation (LID). After 25 years, an 0.5 %/year degradation leaves 88 % of original output.
