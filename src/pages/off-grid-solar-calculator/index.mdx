An off-grid system is a chain: panels charge a battery through a controller, and an inverter turns the battery's DC into AC for the load. Each link has a size, and all four depend on how much energy you use. This calculator sizes them together from nine inputs, each with a default you can change.

## How it works

1. **Array.** The panels must replace the day's energy plus the loss in the battery and inverter. Array (W) = daily load / efficiency / (peak sun hours × performance ratio).
2. **Battery.** Capacity (Ah) = daily load × days of autonomy / (DoD × V × efficiency), the same formula as the <a href="/battery-bank-calculator/">battery bank calculator</a>.
3. **Inverter.** The continuous rating is the peak simultaneous load plus a 25 % margin.
4. **Charge controller.** Output current = installed array watts / battery voltage × 1.25, which is the current an MPPT controller can push into the battery at full sun, with margin.

## Worked example

A cabin uses 5,000 Wh per day with a 2,000 W peak. It has 5 peak sun hours in its worst month, a performance ratio of 0.75, 400 W panels, a 48 V bank, 2 days of autonomy, 50 % DoD and a 0.9 efficiency.

- Energy the array must supply: 5,000 / 0.9 = 5,556 Wh.
- Array: 5,556 / (5 × 0.75) = 1,481 W, which is 3.7 panels, so 4 panels (1,600 W).
- Battery: 5,000 × 2 / (0.5 × 48 × 0.9) = 463 Ah, or 22.2 kWh nominal.
- Inverter: 2,000 × 1.25 = 2,500 W.
- Controller: 1,600 / 48 × 1.25 = 41.7 A, so the next standard size up, such as a 50 A controller.

## Choosing the design month

An off-grid system fails in its worst month, not its average one. In the northern hemisphere, that is usually December. If you design from an annual average of 5 hours and winter gives 2, the batteries will go flat for weeks. Either design for winter, which means a larger and more expensive array that is oversized for summer, or add a generator and accept some fuel use in the weak months.

## What to check next

- **Controller input voltage.** The array's open-circuit voltage, especially at low temperature, must stay below the controller's limit. This depends on how panels are wired in series and parallel, and the calculator does not design strings.
- **Cable sizes.** Battery cables carry large currents. Use the <a href="/solar-wire-size-calculator/">solar DC cable size calculator</a> and check ampacity.
- **Protection.** Fuses or breakers are needed on the battery, the array and the inverter, selected against the applicable code.
- **Surge loads.** Pumps and compressors need more inverter capacity than their running power. See the <a href="/inverter-size-calculator/">inverter size calculator</a>.

## Common mistakes

- **Sizing on average sun.**
- **Sizing the battery on watts instead of watt-hours.**
- **Forgetting that a larger battery needs a larger array to recharge it.**

## Questions

### How many solar panels do I need to run a house off-grid?
It depends entirely on daily energy use and the worst-month sun. A home using 20 kWh per day needs around 6 kW of panels at 5 peak sun hours, and much more in a poor winter.

### Is a generator cheaper than extra batteries?
Often, for the last few days a year. Compare the cost of the extra days of autonomy with the cost of a generator and its fuel.

### Does the calculator assume MPPT or PWM?
The controller figure is a minimum output current. An MPPT controller is the common choice at these sizes.

## Off-grid system component sizing table

Typical sizing relationships for a 48 V lithium off-grid system at different daily load levels:

| Daily load | Array (@ 5 PSH, PR 0.8) | Battery (2 days, 80% DoD) | Inverter | Charge controller |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1,000 Wh | 250 W | 65 Ah | 1,000 W | 10 A MPPT |
| 3,000 Wh | 750 W | 196 Ah | 2,000 W | 20 A MPPT |
| 5,000 Wh | 1,250 W | 326 Ah | 3,000 W | 30 A MPPT |
| 10,000 Wh | 2,500 W | 651 Ah | 5,000 W | 60 A MPPT |
| 20,000 Wh | 5,000 W | 1,302 Ah | 8,000 W | 100 A MPPT |

MPPT current is array Wp / battery voltage. At 48 V: 1,250 W / 48 = 26 A, so the 30 A controller is correct.

## Worked example: rural cabin

A cabin wants to run 500 W of LED lighting (4 h/day), a 100 W laptop (6 h/day), a 150 W fridge (running 8 h/day), and a 750 W water pump (1 h/day). Location: 4.5 peak sun hours.

**Daily energy:**
- Lighting: 500 × 4 = 2,000 Wh
- Laptop: 100 × 6 = 600 Wh
- Fridge: 150 × 8 = 1,200 Wh
- Pump: 750 × 1 = 750 Wh
- **Total: 4,550 Wh/day**

**Array at PR 0.8:** 4,550 / (4.5 × 0.8) = 1,264 W. Use 4 × 325 W panels = 1,300 W.

**Battery at 2 days autonomy, 80% DoD, 48 V:** (4,550 × 2) / (0.8 × 48) = 237 Ah. Use a 48 V, 250 Ah LiFePO4 bank.

**Inverter:** running load 1,500 W + pump start surge ~2,250 W = 3,750 W peak. Use a 4,000 W pure sine wave inverter.

**Charge controller:** 1,300 W / 48 V = 27 A. Use a 40 A MPPT controller.

## Battery chemistry comparison

| Chemistry | Usable DoD | Cycle life | Weight | Cost per kWh |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Flooded lead-acid | 50 % | 300–500 cycles | High | Low |
| AGM | 50–60 % | 400–600 cycles | High | Medium |
| Gel | 50–60 % | 500–800 cycles | High | Medium |
| LiFePO4 | 80–90 % | 2,000–5,000 cycles | Low | High |

LiFePO4 delivers roughly 3–4× more usable energy over its lifetime despite the higher upfront cost, and it handles partial state of charge without sulfation. For off-grid systems used daily, it typically has a lower total cost of ownership over 10 years.

## Panel orientation and seasonal variation

| Latitude | Optimal tilt | Summer PSH | Winter PSH | Annual ratio |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 20° N | 15–20° | 6.0 | 5.0 | 0.83 |
| 35° N | 30–35° | 6.5 | 3.5 | 0.54 |
| 50° N | 45–50° | 6.0 | 2.0 | 0.33 |

At 50° N, winter delivers only a third of summer output. A system sized on annual average will fall 50–60 % short in December. Options: size for winter (overproduces in summer), install a backup generator, or accept grid use in winter.

## More Q&As

### How do I account for seasonal variation in my off-grid system?
Run the calculation using the worst-month PSH for your latitude, not the annual average. For the UK at 51° N that is about 1.5–2 PSH in December, which is roughly one-third of July. Sizing for the worst month means the array overproduces significantly in summer — plan to dump excess to a hot water element or accept it.

### Can I mix panel brands or wattages in one array?
In a single string, all panels should be matched in voltage. In parallel strings, all strings should have equal voltage. Mixing wattages works within those constraints, but mismatched panels lose the benefit of higher-rated units because the lower panel limits the string.

### How do I calculate the charge controller size for a 24 V system?
Divide total panel watts by system voltage: 1,000 W / 24 V = 41.7 A. Choose the next standard size up — a 50 A MPPT controller. Add 25 % for the array's short-circuit current headroom.
