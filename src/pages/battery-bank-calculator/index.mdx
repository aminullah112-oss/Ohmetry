Two questions come up with batteries: how big a bank do I need, and how long will the one I have last. The calculator answers either one. Both use the same four quantities: energy, voltage, usable depth of discharge and inverter efficiency.

## Formulas

<div class="formula">Capacity (Ah) = daily energy (Wh) × days / (DoD × V × efficiency)<br/>Runtime (h) = capacity (Ah) × V × DoD × efficiency / load (W)</div>

**Depth of discharge (DoD)** is the fraction of the capacity you are willing to use. Repeatedly draining a lead-acid battery deeply shortens its life, so about 50 % is a common design limit. Lithium iron phosphate (LiFePO4) batteries are commonly specified for 80 to 90 % or more. Use the figure in the battery's datasheet, not a rule of thumb, because warranty terms often depend on it.

**Efficiency** accounts for the loss in the inverter between the battery and an AC load. Typical inverters convert at 85 to 95 %, and the default here is 0.9. If your load is DC and connects directly, set it to 1.

## Worked example: sizing

A home uses 5,000 Wh per day and wants 2 days of autonomy on a 48 V lead-acid bank limited to 50 % DoD:

Capacity = 5,000 × 2 / (0.5 × 48 × 0.9) = 463 Ah.

That is a nominal 22.2 kWh bank, of which 10 kWh is usable. With a 48 V LiFePO4 bank at 80 % DoD the same autonomy needs 5,000 × 2 / (0.8 × 48 × 0.9) = 289 Ah, which is why lithium banks are much smaller for the same duty.

## Worked example: runtime

A 200 Ah, 48 V battery with 80 % usable depth and a 0.9 inverter runs a 1,000 W load for 200 × 48 × 0.8 × 0.9 / 1,000 = 6.9 hours.

## Choosing system voltage

The same energy at a higher voltage means less current. A 5 kW load draws about 420 A from a 12 V bank but about 104 A from a 48 V bank, so cable sizes, fuses and connector losses shrink considerably. For anything above about 2 kW, 48 V is a standard choice. Batteries are then wired in series to reach the voltage and in parallel to reach the amp-hours: four 12 V batteries in series make 48 V, and a second string in parallel doubles the Ah.

## What the calculator leaves out

- **Temperature.** Batteries deliver less capacity in the cold, and lead-acid more so than lithium. Size for the coldest conditions the bank will see.
- **Discharge rate.** A lead-acid battery gives less than its rated capacity at fast discharge, a behaviour described by Peukert's law. The rated Ah is normally quoted at the 20-hour rate.
- **Ageing.** Capacity falls over the years. Some designers add 10 to 20 % to cover it.
- **Charging.** The bank needs a source that can recharge it between uses. See the <a href="/off-grid-solar-calculator/">off-grid calculator</a>.

## Common mistakes

- **Comparing Ah across voltages.** 100 Ah at 12 V is 1.2 kWh, while 100 Ah at 48 V is 4.8 kWh.

## Questions

### How many batteries do I need?
Divide the required Ah by one battery's Ah for parallel strings, and divide the bank voltage by one battery's voltage for series count.

### How many kWh is a 100 Ah battery?
At 12 V, 1.2 kWh nominal. At 24 V, 2.4 kWh. At 48 V, 4.8 kWh.

### How many days of autonomy should I choose?
One to three days is common for homes. More days cost more, and a generator can be cheaper than the extra batteries.

## Battery sizing reference table

Minimum Ah at 48 V for common off-grid scenarios (LiFePO4, 80% DoD, 90% efficiency):

| Daily energy | 1 day autonomy | 2 days autonomy | 3 days autonomy |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1,000 Wh | 29 Ah | 58 Ah | 87 Ah |
| 2,500 Wh | 72 Ah | 145 Ah | 217 Ah |
| 5,000 Wh | 145 Ah | 289 Ah | 434 Ah |
| 10,000 Wh | 289 Ah | 578 Ah | 868 Ah |
| 20,000 Wh | 578 Ah | 1,157 Ah | 1,736 Ah |

For lead-acid at 50% DoD, multiply all values by 1.6. For 24 V, double all Ah values. For 12 V, multiply by 4.

## Worked example: lead-acid vs LiFePO4

A cabin with 5,000 Wh/day needs 2 days of backup at 48 V.

**Lead-acid (50% DoD, 85% efficiency):**
Ah = (5,000 × 2) / (0.5 × 48 × 0.85) = 490 Ah.
Eight 6V, 250 Ah batteries in series-parallel (48 V, 500 Ah) → 24,000 Wh nominal, 12,000 Wh usable at 50% DoD.

**LiFePO4 (80% DoD, 95% efficiency):**
Ah = (5,000 × 2) / (0.8 × 48 × 0.95) = 275 Ah.
Two 48 V, 150 Ah lithium packs in parallel = 48 V, 300 Ah → 11,520 Wh usable at 80% DoD.

The lithium bank weighs roughly 60 kg vs ~180 kg for lead-acid, occupies half the space, and will outlast the lead-acid bank by 4–8× in cycle life.

## Series vs parallel wiring

| Wiring | Effect | Use when |
|---|---|---|
| Series | Doubles voltage, same Ah | Need higher battery voltage |
| Parallel | Same voltage, doubles Ah | Need more capacity at same voltage |
| Series-parallel | Increases both | Need both higher voltage and more Ah |

For a 48 V system using 12 V batteries: wire 4 in series. For 300 Ah using 100 Ah batteries: wire 3 in parallel. For 48 V, 300 Ah: wire 4 strings of 3 × 12 V in series, the 3 strings in parallel (4S3P).

## State of charge and battery health

Depth of discharge is cumulative — a lead-acid bank cycled to 70% DoD every day degrades faster than one cycled to 50%. The 50% DoD limit for lead-acid is a lifetime cost optimisation, not a hard cutoff. LiFePO4 handles 80% DoD daily with minimal degradation because lithium intercalation chemistry does not cause sulfation.

A battery management system (BMS) is essential for lithium banks. It prevents over-discharge, overcharge, cell imbalance, and over-temperature. For lead-acid, a temperature-compensated charge controller achieves the same goal.

## More Q&As

### Can I add more batteries to my existing bank later?
For lead-acid: only add new batteries in a full set, all the same age and type. Mixing old and new batteries causes the old ones to drag down the new ones' capacity. For LiFePO4: check that the BMS supports the new capacity and that all batteries are the same cell chemistry and voltage.

### What is C-rate, and why does it matter for battery sizing?
C-rate is the discharge current as a multiple of the battery's capacity. A 100 Ah battery at 1C discharges at 100 A. Lead-acid batteries lose capacity at high discharge rates (Peukert effect) — a 100 Ah battery at 2C may deliver only 70 Ah. LiFePO4 is much less affected. If your load draws high current, size the bank for the C-rate, not just the Wh.

### How do I wire a 48 V lithium pack from 3.2 V cells?
LiFePO4 cells have a nominal voltage of 3.2 V. 48 V nominal = 15 cells in series (actual voltage: 15 × 3.2 = 48 V; fully charged 15 × 3.65 = 54.75 V). Wire multiple 15S modules in parallel for more amp-hours.
