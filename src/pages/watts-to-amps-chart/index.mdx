This chart gives the current drawn by a load of known wattage on common DC and single-phase AC voltages. It assumes a power factor of 1.0, which is exact for DC and for resistive AC loads such as heaters and incandescent lamps. For electronic or motor loads on AC, the current is higher by 1 / PF.

## The formula

<div class="formula">I = P / V<br/>AC with power factor: I = P / (V × PF)</div>

I is in amps, P in watts and V in volts. For three-phase loads and for any wattage not shown, use the <a href="/watts-to-amps-calculator/">watts to amps calculator</a>.

## Common loads

These are typical figures. Check the nameplate on your own equipment, because wattages vary.

- **1,200 W** (a microwave or a small heater) is 10 A at 120 V and 5 A at 240 V.
- **1,440 W** is 12 A at 120 V. A 12 A load on 120 V is a common current for slow electric vehicle charging.
- **1,500 W** (a typical portable heater setting) is 12.5 A at 120 V, close to the continuous limit of a 15 A circuit.
- **1,800 W** is 15 A at 120 V, the full rating of a 15 A breaker.
- **1,100 W** is 9.17 A at 120 V and 4.58 A at 240 V.
- **1,300 W** is 10.8 A at 120 V and 5.42 A at 240 V.
- **1,400 W** is 11.7 A at 120 V and 5.83 A at 240 V.
- **2,200 W** is 18.3 A at 120 V and 9.17 A at 240 V.
- **3,300 W** is 27.5 A at 120 V and 13.75 A at 240 V.
- **4,500 W** (a common water heater element) is 18.75 A at 240 V and 37.5 A at 120 V.
- **5,500 W** is 22.9 A at 240 V.
- **8,000 W** is 33.3 A at 240 V.
- **10,000 W** is 41.7 A at 240 V.

Add 25 % when the load runs for three hours or more, to size the breaker. The <a href="/breaker-amps-to-watts-chart/">breaker amps to watts chart</a> does that for you.

## Why low-voltage loads draw so much current

The same power at one tenth of the voltage needs ten times the current. A 1,500 W load draws 12.5 A at 120 V and 125 A at 12 V. The cable has to carry that current, and its voltage drop eats a bigger share of a low supply voltage. This is why 12 V systems need thick, short cables and why larger systems move to 24 V or 48 V.

## Worked example: an inverter on a 12 V battery

A 2,000 W inverter load at 12 V draws 2,000 / 12 = 167 A according to the chart. The inverter is not 100 % efficient. At 90 % efficiency the battery supplies 2,000 / 0.9 = 2,222 W, which is 185 A. That is the current the battery, fuse and cable must handle, and it is why the cable between a battery and a large inverter is so heavy. For the cable size, use the <a href="/solar-wire-size-calculator/">DC cable size calculator</a>.

## Things the chart leaves out

- **Power factor.** On AC, a 1,000 W load at PF 0.8 draws 1,000 / (120 × 0.8) = 10.4 A at 120 V, not the 8.33 A in the table.
- **Motor starting.** Motors draw several times their running current for a moment when they start.
- **Efficiency.** Inverters, chargers and power supplies draw more input power than the output they deliver.
- **Continuous operation.** Wiring and breaker sizing rules add margin for continuous loads. See <a href="/how-to-size-a-circuit-breaker/">how to size a circuit breaker</a>.

## Questions

### How many amps is 1,000 watts?
8.33 A at 120 V, 4.17 A at 240 V, 83.3 A at 12 V and 20.8 A at 48 V, for a power factor of 1.0.

### How many watts is 15 amps at 120 V?
1,800 W. The 1,800 W row shows 15.0 A at 120 V.

### How many amps is 1,200 watts?
10 A at 120 V and 5 A at 240 V, for a power factor of 1.0. At 12 V it is 100 A.

### How many amps is 1,400 watts?
11.7 A at 120 V and 5.83 A at 240 V. At 12 V it is 117 A.

### How many amps is 4,500 watts at 240 V?
18.75 A. For a water heater element that runs for hours, use 125 % of that, 23.4 A, to choose the breaker, which points to a 30 A breaker. See the <a href="/breaker-amps-to-watts-chart/">breaker chart</a>.

### Does the chart work for a heat pump or a motor?
Not directly. Those loads have a power factor below 1 and a high starting current. Use the nameplate current or the NEC table value for the motor.

## Low-voltage DC loads — 12 V and 48 V systems

DC loads convert differently. No power factor applies (assume 1.0 unless stated), but efficiency losses in DC-DC converters or inverters add to the draw.

| Load | Watts | 12 V current | 48 V current |
|---|---|---|---|
| LED cabin light | 5 W | 0.42 A | 0.10 A |
| USB device charging | 20 W | 1.7 A | 0.42 A |
| Car refrigerator | 60 W | 5.0 A | 1.25 A |
| Ham radio transceiver | 150 W | 12.5 A | 3.1 A |
| Inverter (600 W output) | 667 W | 55.6 A | 13.9 A |
| Inverter (3,000 W output) | 3,333 W | 278 A (too large for 12 V) | 69.4 A |

A 3,000 W load on 12 V draws 278 A, which requires 300 A+ cable and connectors. That is why off-grid systems use 48 V or higher: the 48 V equivalent is 69 A — a manageable current with standard wiring.

## Circuit breaker sizing from wattage

For a **continuous load** (runs 3 hours or more), NEC 210.20(A) requires the breaker to be rated at least 125% of the load current. The chart values are running currents, not breaker sizes:

| Load | 240 V running amps | 125% | Standard breaker |
|---|---|---|---|
| Water heater 4,500 W | 18.75 A | 23.4 A | 30 A |
| Electric dryer 5,000 W | 20.8 A | 26.1 A | 30 A |
| Level 2 EV 7,600 W | 31.7 A | 39.6 A | 40 A |
| Electric range 8,000 W | 33.3 A | 41.7 A | 50 A |
| Hot tub 6,000 W | 25.0 A | 31.3 A | 40 A |

Standard breaker sizes (NEC 240.6(A)): 15, 20, 25, 30, 35, 40, 45, 50, 60, 70, 80, 90, 100 A.

## More Q&As

### Why does the 120 V current double compared to 240 V?
Power = Voltage × Current, so for the same watts, halving the voltage doubles the current. A 1,500 W heater draws 12.5 A at 120 V but only 6.25 A at 240 V. Manufacturers design high-draw appliances for 240 V to keep the current and conductor size manageable.

### How many watts can a 15 A circuit supply continuously?
At 120 V, a 15 A circuit is limited to 80% = 12 A continuous under NEC 210.20(A), giving 12 × 120 = 1,440 W. At 240 V: 12 × 240 = 2,880 W. The 80% rule (or 125% applied to load current) is the same either way.

### Can I run a 1,500 W heater and a coffee maker on the same 15 A circuit?
Only if neither runs continuously. A 1,500 W heater is 12.5 A at 120 V; a 1,200 W coffee maker is 10 A. Together: 22.5 A, which immediately trips a 15 A breaker. Even at reduced combined loads, a 15 A breaker is not meant to supply both continuously. Use separate circuits.
