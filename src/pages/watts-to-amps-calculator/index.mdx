Watts tell you how much power a load uses. Amps tell you how much current it draws, and that is the figure that sizes a breaker and a cable. The conversion depends on the voltage, and on AC it also depends on the power factor, so the same wattage draws very different currents on different supplies.

## Formulas

<div class="formula">DC: I = P / V<br/>AC single-phase: I = P / (V × PF)<br/>AC three-phase: I = P / (√3 × V<sub>LL</sub> × PF)</div>

I is current in amps, P is real power in watts, V is voltage and PF is the power factor from 0 to 1. For three-phase, V<sub>LL</sub> is the line-to-line voltage and I is the current in each line, assuming a balanced load. If your power is in kilowatts, use the <a href="/kw-to-amps-calculator/">kW to amps calculator</a>, which is the same formula with the power entered in kW.

## Worked examples

A 1,500 W heater on a 120 V single-phase supply, power factor 1:

I = 1,500 / 120 = 12.5 A. On 240 V the same heater draws 6.25 A.

A 3,000 W single-phase motor load on 240 V at a power factor of 0.9:

I = 3,000 / (240 × 0.9) = 13.89 A.

A 10,000 W three-phase load on 400 V at 0.8 power factor:

I = 10,000 / (1.732 × 400 × 0.8) = 18.04 A.

## Current for common loads

At a power factor of 1, in amps:

| Power | 120 V | 208 V | 230 V | 240 V | 277 V |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1,000 W | 8.33 | 4.81 | 4.35 | 4.17 | 3.61 |
| 1,500 W | 12.50 | 7.21 | 6.52 | 6.25 | 5.42 |
| 2,000 W | 16.67 | 9.62 | 8.70 | 8.33 | 7.22 |

On a 12 V DC system, 100 W draws 8.33 A. At 24 V it draws 4.17 A and at 48 V 2.08 A, which is why higher-voltage DC systems need thinner cable for the same power.

## From amps to a breaker

Once you have the current, the breaker and the cable follow. Under the NEC, a load that runs for three hours or more is a continuous load and the circuit must be rated for 125 % of it. The 1,500 W heater above draws 12.5 A, and 12.5 × 1.25 = 15.6 A, so a 15 A breaker is too small and the next standard size is 20 A. The <a href="/breaker-size-calculator/">breaker size calculator</a> does this step for you.

## Common mistakes

- **Using the line-to-neutral voltage on three-phase.** With the √3 formula, use the line-to-line voltage, such as 400 V or 208 V. Using 230 V understates the power, or overstates the current, by a factor of 1.732.
- **Ignoring power factor.** A motor load at 0.8 draws 25 % more current than the same watts at unity. Take the power factor from the nameplate or from a measurement.
- **Confusing watts and volt-amps.** A label that gives VA, as on many UPS units, is apparent power. The real power in watts is lower by the power factor.
- **Sizing from the running current alone.** Motors and compressors draw several times their running current for a moment at start, so a breaker or a generator sized to the running figure may trip.

## Questions

### How many amps is 1,000 watts?
At 120 V it is 8.33 A. At 230 V it is 4.35 A, and on a 12 V DC supply it is 83.3 A. The answer always depends on the voltage.

### How many watts can a 15 A, 120 V circuit carry?
15 × 120 = 1,800 W at most. For a continuous load the practical limit is 80 % of that, 1,440 W, because the breaker must be 125 % of a continuous load.

### Do watts to amps and kW to amps give the same answer?
Yes, if you enter the same power. 1,000 W is 1 kW, so both calculators agree.

### Why does the power factor not appear for DC?
DC has no phase shift between voltage and current, so the power is simply volts times amps.

### How many amps is a 1,500 W space heater?
On a 120 V circuit: 1,500 / 120 = 12.5 A. On 240 V: 6.25 A. Heaters are resistive (power factor 1), so voltage is the only variable.

### How many amps does a 100 W light bulb use?
A 100 W incandescent on 120 V draws 0.83 A. An LED delivering the same light uses roughly 10–15 W, drawing 0.08–0.13 A.

### How many amps is 2,000 watts on 240 V?
2,000 / 240 = 8.33 A for a resistive load. For an inductive load at 0.85 power factor: 2,000 / (240 × 0.85) = 9.80 A.

### How do I size a circuit for a 2,000 W load?
If the load runs continuously: 2,000 W / 120 V = 16.67 A. Apply the 125 % rule: 16.67 × 1.25 = 20.8 A. Use a 25 A breaker (next standard size above 20.8 A) and a conductor rated for at least 25 A — 10 AWG at 75 °C covers 35 A and passes comfortably.

### What is the current draw of a 5,000 W generator output?
At 240 V single-phase: 5,000 / 240 = 20.8 A. That is the output current. The generator's input from the engine is higher by the alternator efficiency (typically 85–95 %).

## Power factor — what it means in practice

Power factor is the ratio of real power (watts) to apparent power (volt-amps). A load with a power factor of 0.8 draws 25 % more current than the same wattage at unity power factor, because part of the current flows back and forth without doing work.

**Typical power factors by load type:**

| Load | Typical power factor |
|---|---|
| Resistive heaters, incandescent lighting | 1.00 |
| LED luminaires | 0.90–0.95 |
| Fluorescent lighting with electronic ballast | 0.90–0.95 |
| Induction motors at full load | 0.85–0.92 |
| Induction motors at 50 % load | 0.70–0.80 |
| Variable-speed drives | 0.95–0.98 |
| UPS systems at load | 0.90–0.99 |
| Welding equipment | 0.50–0.70 |

For lighting and heating circuits, power factor is effectively 1.0 and the simple P / V formula is accurate. For motor loads, nameplate power factor must be used or the current will be understated. Many industrial meters show power factor directly; for a home circuit a clamp meter measuring both watts and volt-amps will give it.

## Sizing a circuit from watts — the full NEC sequence

Converting watts to amps is step one of sizing a circuit. The full sequence under NEC Article 210:

1. **Calculate the running current**: I = P / (V × PF) for single-phase AC.
2. **Apply the continuous-load multiplier**: if the load runs for three hours or more, multiply by 1.25 per NEC 210.20(A).
3. **Select the breaker**: next standard size at or above the multiplied current (NEC 240.6(A)). Standard sizes: 15, 20, 25, 30, 35, 40, 50, 60, 70, 80, 90, 100 A.
4. **Select the conductor**: ampacity from NEC Table 310.12 must equal or exceed the breaker size for most circuits. 12 AWG copper covers 20 A at 75 °C, 10 AWG covers 35 A, 8 AWG covers 50 A.
5. **Check voltage drop**: for runs over 50 ft, check that the drop stays under 3 % at the breaker rating. Use the <a href="/voltage-drop-calculator/">voltage drop calculator</a>.

**Example — 2,400 W continuous resistive load on 240 V:**

Running current: 2,400 / 240 = 10 A. Continuous multiplier: 10 × 1.25 = 12.5 A. Breaker: 15 A (next standard above 12.5). Conductor: 14 AWG at 75 °C covers 15 A — acceptable. For a 100 ft run: loop = 200 ft, 14 AWG resistance at 75 °C = 3.071 Ω/1,000 ft × 0.2 = 0.614 Ω, voltage drop = 10 × 0.614 = 6.14 V or 2.6 % — passes 3 % target.

## Real power, apparent power and VA ratings

Many devices — UPS units, generators, transformers — are rated in volt-amps (VA) rather than watts. VA is the apparent power, which includes both the real power doing work and the reactive power circulating in the system.

<div class="formula">VA = V × I<br/>W = VA × PF<br/>kVAR = √(kVA² − kW²)</div>

A 1,000 VA UPS at a power factor of 0.9 delivers 900 W of real power. The current it draws from the wall is 1,000 / 120 = 8.33 A, not 900 / 120 = 7.5 A. Sizing a UPS circuit from the watt rating understates the current. Always use the VA figure for circuit sizing.

Generators are rated in kVA at a stated power factor (typically 0.8 lagging). A 10 kVA generator at 0.8 PF delivers 8 kW. If the connected load totals 8 kW at 0.95 PF, the apparent power is 8 / 0.95 = 8.42 kVA — the generator has headroom. If the same 8 kW load is at 0.7 PF, the apparent power is 11.4 kVA, which overloads a 10 kVA generator even though the watts are within rating.

## Low-voltage DC systems

On DC systems at 12 V, 24 V or 48 V, the same wattage produces much larger currents than on mains AC. This is why cable sizing on solar, RV, marine and telecom systems is critical.

| Power | 12 V | 24 V | 48 V | 120 V | 240 V |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 100 W | 8.33 A | 4.17 A | 2.08 A | 0.83 A | 0.42 A |
| 500 W | 41.7 A | 20.8 A | 10.4 A | 4.17 A | 2.08 A |
| 1,000 W | 83.3 A | 41.7 A | 20.8 A | 8.33 A | 4.17 A |
| 3,000 W | 250 A | 125 A | 62.5 A | 25 A | 12.5 A |

A 3,000 W inverter running from a 12 V battery bank draws 250 A — a cable and connection size that surprises many first-time installers. At 48 V the same inverter draws 62.5 A, which is why 48 V is the standard bus voltage for larger off-grid and backup systems.
