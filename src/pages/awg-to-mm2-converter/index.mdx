American Wire Gauge is a logarithmic scale: each step down in gauge number increases the cross-section by about 26%, and every three steps roughly doubles it. Metric cables are specified by cross-sectional area in mm², in a different set of preferred sizes, so a conversion is always approximate in one direction.

## Formula

<div class="formula">d = 0.127 mm × 92<sup>(36 − n) / 39</sup><br/>A = π / 4 × d²</div>

n is the gauge number. For 1/0, 2/0, 3/0 and 4/0, use n = 0, −1, −2 and −3. The diameter is that of a solid round conductor. The definition comes from the standard that fixes 4/0 at 0.46 inch and 36 at 0.005 inch.

## Worked example

AWG 10: d = 0.127 × 92<sup>(26/39)</sup> = 0.127 × 20.38 = 2.588 mm.

A = π / 4 × 2.588² = 5.26 mm².

AWG 4/0: d = 11.684 mm, A = 107.2 mm². Both agree with the widely published gauge tables.

## Reading the result

The calculator returns the nearest AWG to a given mm² size. That is a match by area, not a statement that the cables are interchangeable. If your design requires at least 6 mm², AWG 9 (6.63 mm²) is the nearest, but AWG 10 (5.26 mm²) is smaller than the requirement. Round to the larger conductor when the area is a minimum.

Two further points matter when you compare cables:

- **Stranding changes the numbers slightly.** The formula gives the solid-conductor area. A stranded cable with the same AWG number has a slightly larger overall diameter and a copper area a little under the nominal value. Manufacturers' data sheets are the authority.
- **Area is not ampacity.** Allowable current depends on insulation temperature rating, installation method, ambient temperature and the governing code, which differ between NEC and IEC regimes. This page converts sizes only. Do not select a cable from the area alone.

## Metric preferred sizes

IEC 60228 preferred conductor sizes include 1.5, 2.5, 4, 6, 10, 16, 25, 35, 50, 70, 95, 120, 150, 185 and 240 mm². AWG sizes rarely land on one of these, which is why a design moved from one system to the other usually needs a fresh calculation and not just a conversion.

## Large conductors in kcmil

Above 4/0, American practice quotes size in kcmil, thousands of circular mils. One kcmil is 0.5067 mm², so a 250 kcmil conductor has a nominal area of about 126.7 mm². This calculator covers AWG sizes from 4/0 up to 30 AWG.

## Substituting one size for another

When a spec calls for a metric cable and only AWG is on hand, or the other way round, choose the size with the same or larger area. A smaller conductor has more resistance per metre, so for the same current it loses more voltage and runs hotter. The length also matters: a short run of a slightly undersized cable may be acceptable on paper, but the voltage drop and the code limits have to be checked in each case.

## Questions

### What is the metric equivalent of 12 AWG?
About 3.31 mm². The nearest common metric size is 4 mm², which is larger, while 2.5 mm² is smaller.

### Is AWG 14 the same as 2.5 mm²?
No. AWG 14 is 2.08 mm² and 2.5 mm² is between AWG 13 and 14. In practice they are treated as roughly comparable for light circuits, but the allowable current differs by code.

### Why do larger AWG numbers mean thinner wire?
The number counts the drawing steps through dies used in manufacture. More steps give thinner wire.

### How do I read 4/0?
It is spoken "four-aught" and is the largest standard AWG size before sizes are quoted in kcmil. It is 107.2 mm².
