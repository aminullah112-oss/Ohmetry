Voltage drop is the voltage lost along a cable because of its resistance, and for longer AC runs its reactance. Too much of it makes motors run hot, lights dim and electronics reset. This calculator gives the drop for a cable you have already chosen. To work the other way and find the smallest cable for a limit, use the <a href="/cable-size-calculator/">cable size calculator</a> or the <a href="/solar-wire-size-calculator/">solar DC cable size calculator</a>.

## Voltage drop formulas, single-phase and three-phase

<div class="formula">Two-wire or DC: ΔV = 2 × I × L × R<br/>Three-phase: ΔV = √3 × I × L × R<br/>With reactance: ΔV = k × I × L × (R cos φ + X sin φ)<br/>R = ρ / A</div>

I is the load current, L the one-way length, R the conductor resistance per unit length, ρ the resistivity and A the cross-section. The factor k is 2 for two wires (out and back) and √3 for a balanced three-phase circuit, where the result is a line-to-line voltage. For the full derivation and more examples, see the <a href="/voltage-drop-formula/">voltage drop formula guide</a>.

## Worked example

A 230 V single-phase circuit carries 20 A over 30 m of 4 mm² copper at an operating temperature of 70 °C. Resistivity at 70 °C is 0.020629 Ω·mm²/m.

ΔV = 2 × 30 × 20 × 0.020629 / 4 = 6.19 V, which is 2.7 % of 230 V. The load sees 223.8 V.

Now a three-phase 400 V feeder carrying 100 A over 50 m of 25 mm² copper at 20 °C: ΔV = 1.732 × 100 × 50 × 0.017241 / 25 = 5.97 V, or 1.49 % of 400 V.

## Example in US units (feet and AWG)

Choose **US (ft, AWG)** in the calculator for NEC-style inputs. A 20 A load sits 100 ft from the panel on a 120 V single-phase circuit, copper at 75 °C:

| Wire | Voltage drop | Percent of 120 V |
|---|---|---|
| 12 AWG | 7.73 V | 6.4 % |
| 10 AWG | 4.86 V | 4.1 % |
| 8 AWG | 3.06 V | 2.5 % |

Only 8 AWG meets the commonly used 3 % target. The NEC's 3 % and 5 % figures are recommendations, not requirements, unless your specification adopts them. The <a href="/nec-voltage-drop-guide/">NEC voltage drop guide</a> explains where they come from and works through the same circuit with the rounded K values used by hand.

## Temperature, resistance and reactance

Conductor resistance rises about 0.39 % per °C for copper, so a cable at 70 °C has roughly 20 % more resistance than the same cable at 20 °C. The default of 70 °C matches PVC insulation at its rated load, and 90 °C suits XLPE. Using the cold value understates the drop on a heavily loaded circuit.

For small cables, resistance dominates and the resistive formula is enough. For larger conductors, from roughly 25 mm² upward, the reactance of the cable adds to the drop at normal power factors. If you enter a reactance from the cable data sheet in Ω/km, the calculator applies the full formula using the power factor you give. Without a reactance value, the calculation is resistance only and ignores power factor, which can understate the drop for large cables carrying an inductive load.

## What drop is acceptable?

Limits come from the regulations and the equipment. As examples, BS 7671 gives 3 % for lighting and 5 % for other uses, and the informational notes in the NEC suggest 3 % on a branch circuit and 5 % for feeder and branch circuit combined. Those are recommendations in some codes and requirements in others. Motors have a further limit during starting, when the drop is much larger because of the starting current, and some equipment states its own tolerance.

## What the result does not cover

- **Starting current.** Motor starting draws several times the running current and the drop is proportionally larger for those seconds.
- **Ampacity.** A cable that passes the drop test can still overheat. Check its current rating as well.
- **Unbalanced three-phase loads.** The formula assumes balanced currents.
- **Long DC or low-voltage runs.** The same drop is a much larger percentage at 12 V or 24 V than at 230 V.

## Common mistakes

- **Using the round-trip length as the one-way length.** The calculator doubles it for you.
- **Using 20 °C resistivity for a fully loaded cable.**
- **Taking the percentage against the wrong voltage.** On three-phase, use line-to-line voltage with the √3 form.

## Questions

### What is the 3 phase voltage drop formula?
ΔV = √3 × I × L × (R cos φ + X sin φ), with R and X the resistance and reactance per unit length, L the one-way length and the result a line-to-line voltage. For small cables, drop the reactance term and use ΔV = √3 × I × L × R. To get a percentage, divide by the line-to-line voltage, for example 400 V.

### How do I calculate voltage drop for an armoured (SWA) cable?
The method is the same: use the cable's resistance and reactance per metre from its data sheet. BS 7671 Appendix 4 gives the same information as millivolts per amp per metre (mV/A/m), where you multiply the value by the current and the length in metres, then divide by 1,000 to get volts. The calculator here uses the resistivity of the conductor and, if you enter it, the reactance.

### How do I calculate voltage drop in a three-phase circuit?
Multiply √3 by the current, the one-way length in kilometres and the resistance per kilometre. Add the reactance term if the cable is large or the load is inductive.

### How do I convert the drop to a percentage?
Divide it by the supply voltage and multiply by 100.

### Does cable length or size matter more?
Both are in the formula directly. Doubling the length doubles the drop, and doubling the cross-section halves it.

### How much voltage drop is acceptable for an EV charger?
A Level 2 EVSE draws up to 48 A continuously. On a 240 V circuit, 3 % drop is 7.2 V, leaving the charger at 232.8 V. Most EVSEs tolerate down to 208 V, so the headroom is wide, but a large drop means hotter cables and wasted energy. NEC 625.17 does not set a specific limit, so apply the same 3 % branch-circuit recommendation as any continuous load.

### What is the voltage drop on a 100 ft run of 12 AWG at 20 A?
At 75 °C (the NEC design temperature), 12 AWG copper has a resistance of about 1.93 Ω per 1,000 ft. The loop for a 100 ft run is 200 ft, so resistance is 0.386 Ω. Voltage drop is 20 × 0.386 = 7.72 V, which is 6.4 % of 120 V — well above the 3 % guide. The fix is 10 AWG (0.243 Ω loop, 4.86 V, 4.1 %) or 8 AWG (0.153 Ω loop, 3.06 V, 2.5 %).

### Does voltage drop affect motor performance?
Yes. Motors draw several times their running current during starting, so the momentary voltage drop at starting is much larger than the running drop. A motor that starts at 600 % FLA on a circuit with 2 % running drop will see 12 % drop at starting. Many motors will not start below 85–90 % of rated voltage, and the torque is proportional to V², so a 10 % low voltage cuts starting torque by roughly 19 %. For motor circuits, check both the running drop and the starting drop.

## EV charger and long feeder circuits

EV chargers, subpanels in detached garages, and outbuilding feeders are the most common sources of large voltage drop in residential work. They share two features: long one-way runs (often 50–150 ft or more) and high continuous current.

**EV charger example.** A 50 A, 240 V circuit (40 A charger on a 50 A breaker) runs 80 ft from the main panel to the garage. Using 6 AWG copper at 75 °C (R = 0.491 Ω/1,000 ft), the loop is 160 ft and the resistance is 0.0785 Ω. Voltage drop: 50 × 0.0785 = 3.93 V, or 1.6 % of 240 V — acceptable. At 150 ft one-way the loop doubles to 300 ft, resistance is 0.147 Ω, and the drop is 7.37 V or 3.1 %. Borderline. Upgrading to 4 AWG (0.308 Ω/1,000 ft) drops it to 4.62 V or 1.9 %.

**Outbuilding feeder.** A detached shop 200 ft from the house runs a 100 A, 240 V feeder. At 100 ft, 2 AWG copper (0.194 Ω/1,000 ft) has a 400 ft loop resistance of 0.0776 Ω and a drop of 7.76 V (3.2 % of 240 V). But at 200 ft the loop is 800 ft and the drop is 15.5 V (6.5 %) — well over the 5 % total guideline. Moving to 1/0 AWG (0.122 Ω/1,000 ft) brings it to 9.75 V or 4.1 %. The <a href="/cable-size-calculator/">cable size calculator</a> works this in reverse: enter the target drop and it returns the minimum conductor size.

## How to reduce voltage drop

When the calculated drop exceeds the target, four options exist — in rough order of cost:

1. **Increase conductor size.** Each AWG step (or the next standard metric size) reduces resistance by about 25 %. Going up two steps halves the drop.
2. **Reduce circuit length.** Relocating the panel or sub-panel closer to the load is a one-time structural fix that eliminates the problem permanently. Not always practical.
3. **Increase the supply voltage.** A 120 V circuit that drops 5 % drops only 2.5 % if re-wired as 240 V at the same current, because the percentage is against twice the voltage. This works for loads that accept 240 V (motors, heaters, EV chargers).
4. **Reduce the current.** Splitting one large circuit into two shorter ones, or reducing the load, cuts the drop proportionally. Used when upsizing the wire is not cost-effective.

## Voltage drop versus ampacity — two separate checks

A conductor must pass two independent tests:

| Check | Limit source | What it protects |
|---|---|---|
| Ampacity | NEC Table 310.12 / Table 310.16 | Insulation temperature |
| Voltage drop | NEC FPN (recommendation) | Equipment performance |

A 10 AWG copper conductor in conduit has a 75 °C ampacity of 35 A under NEC Table 310.12. It passes the ampacity test for a 30 A load. But if the run is 150 ft at 30 A on a 120 V circuit, the loop resistance is 0.584 Ω and the drop is 17.5 V (14.6 %). The wire is safe thermally but the load performs poorly. Sizing from ampacity alone without checking voltage drop is the most common error on long residential and commercial runs.

The reverse error also occurs: upsizing the conductor to control voltage drop can produce a conductor rated far above the breaker size, which is usually fine but requires checking NEC 240.4(B) for the overcurrent protection sizing rules.
