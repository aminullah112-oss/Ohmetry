A generator has to do two jobs: carry everything that runs together, and ride through the surge when a motor starts. Size it for the first and it may stall on the second. This calculator checks both and tells you which one set the size.

## Formulas

<div class="formula">Running load = Σ (watts × quantity)<br/>Extra starting load = largest of watts × (start multiplier − 1)<br/>kW for running = running load × (1 + margin)<br/>kW for starting = (running load + extra starting load) / starting capability<br/>Required kW = larger of the two / site derating factor<br/>kVA = kW / generator power factor</div>

Enter each load once, with its running watts, quantity and a start multiplier. Use 1 for loads that don't surge, such as lights and heaters. Induction motors often start at three to seven times their running power, and compressors and well pumps sit at the top of that range. The nameplate or the data sheet gives a better figure than a rule of thumb.

## Worked example

A house has 2,000 W of lighting and outlets, two refrigerators at 150 W each with a start multiplier of 3, and a 1,000 W well pump with a start multiplier of 3.

- **Running load:** 2,000 + 300 + 1,000 = 3,300 W.
- **Extra starting load:** the pump adds 1,000 × 2 = 2,000 W, and a refrigerator adds only 300 W, so 2,000 W.
- **For running:** 3,300 × 1.25 = 4.125 kW.
- **For starting:** 3,300 + 2,000 = 5.3 kW.
- **Required:** 5.3 kW, so motor starting sets the size. At a power factor of 0.8 that is 6.6 kVA, or 27.6 A at 240 V single-phase.

Choose the next standard size up from the result. With a site derating factor of 0.9, the example becomes 5.9 kW.

## The four settings

**Margin on running load.** Running a generator at its limit all day is hard on it. A margin of 0.25 keeps the continuous load at 80 % of the rating and leaves room for loads added later.

**Starting capability.** The default of 1 means the generator's rated kW must cover the whole starting peak, which is the conservative reading. Some sets, especially those with an oversized alternator or a motor-starting option, can start more than their rated kW for a short time with some voltage dip. If the manufacturer states that capability, enter it here. Otherwise leave it at 1.

**Site derating.** Engines produce less power at altitude and in heat. Manufacturers publish the derating for their sets, so enter the factor from the data sheet. It reduces the usable rating, so the calculator divides by it.

**Power factor.** Generators are rated in kVA at a stated power factor, usually 0.8. A set rated 100 kVA at 0.8 delivers 80 kW. The calculator converts the required kW to kVA with the value you enter.

## What the calculator assumes

- **Motors start one at a time.** If several start together, the extra starting load is larger. Add them in one row, or use a staggered start with a controller.
- **Linear loads.** Drives, rectifiers and other electronic loads can need a larger alternator because of harmonic heating.
- **Rating class.** Standby, prime and continuous ratings differ, as defined in ISO 8528. A standby rating is for limited hours of use, so check the one you plan to rely on.

## Code and practical points

Standby systems in the US fall under NEC Articles 700, 701 and 702, depending on whether they serve emergency, legally required or optional loads. Article 702 covers optional standby systems and sets requirements for capacity and transfer equipment, so check the current text and your inspector. A transfer switch is also sized for the load it carries. Fuel supply, run time and the exhaust and ventilation requirements are separate design questions.

## Common mistakes

- **Adding up nameplate ratings of everything.** Not all loads run at once, so use the realistic running set.
- **Forgetting the starting surge.**
- **Using kVA and kW as the same number.**
- **Ignoring altitude and heat.**

## Questions

### What size generator do I need to run a house?
It depends on the loads you want to run together. Enter them, and the calculator shows both the running need and the starting need.

### Why is the required kW higher than my running load?
The margin and the starting surge both add to it. The calculator shows which one governs.

### Is a 7 kW generator enough for a 5 kW load?
Often, if the 5 kW does not include a large motor start. Check the starting peak too.

## Generator sizing table — common load scenarios

Approximate minimum generator size for typical applications. These assume a 25 % running margin and a motor starting multiplier of 3 for pumps and air conditioners.

| Application | Running load (kW) | Starting peak (kW) | Minimum generator |
|---|---|---|---|
| Essential home (lights, fridge, well pump) | 3–4 kW | 5–7 kW | 7–8 kW |
| Whole-house backup (US average) | 8–12 kW | 14–20 kW | 20 kW |
| Small workshop (tools + lighting) | 5–8 kW | 10–15 kW | 15 kW |
| Central air conditioner (3-ton) | 3.5 kW | 10–14 kW | 14 kW |
| Well pump (1 hp, 230 V) | 0.75 kW | 2.25 kW | 3 kW |
| Small commercial (retail, 400 V 3-ph) | 15–30 kW | 25–50 kW | 50 kW |

A central air conditioner is often the hardest load to start. A 3-ton (10.5 kW thermal) unit may draw only 3.5 kW running but needs 10–14 kW for the first few seconds. Generators that otherwise handle the full house load can stall on the AC start alone.

## Load list worked example — home backup

A house wants to run essential loads only during an outage:

| Load | Running watts | Qty | Total (W) | Start multiplier | Start peak (W) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Lighting (LED) | 100 W | 10 | 1,000 W | 1 | 1,000 W |
| Refrigerator | 150 W | 1 | 150 W | 3 | 450 W |
| Chest freezer | 100 W | 1 | 100 W | 3 | 300 W |
| Well pump (1 hp) | 750 W | 1 | 750 W | 3 | 2,250 W |
| Microwave | 1,200 W | 1 | 1,200 W | 1 | 1,200 W |

Running total: 3,200 W. Largest starting surge: well pump at 2,250 W extra. Total during pump start: 3,200 + (2,250 − 750) = 4,700 W.

For running: 3,200 × 1.25 = 4,000 W. For starting: 4,700 W governs. At a power factor of 0.8: 4,700 / 0.8 = 5,875 VA = 5.875 kVA. Choose the next standard generator size: **7 kVA or 7 kW**, which at 0.8 PF delivers 5.6 kW — just enough. For comfort and to run the microwave while the pump starts, a 10 kW set is a better fit.

## Standby, prime and continuous ratings

Generators carry different output ratings for different duty cycles, defined in ISO 8528:

| Rating | Typical use | Hours per year | Load variation |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Standby** | Emergency backup | ≤ 200 h/yr | Variable, up to 100 % |
| **Prime** | Main power source | Unlimited | Variable, avg ≤ 70 % |
| **Continuous** | Base load, always on | Unlimited | Constant |

A standby-rated set produces more power but only for limited annual hours. A prime-rated set is derated, typically 10 % below standby. A continuous set is derated further — typically 70–80 % of standby.

If the generator is for emergency backup only, the standby rating is the right one. If it is the primary power source for a remote site, use the prime or continuous rating. Using a standby-rated set at continuous duty wears it out early.

## Transfer switch and NEC considerations

A generator requires a transfer switch to isolate the building from the utility before the generator connects — feeding a generator into a live grid can injure utility workers. Under NEC 702 (optional standby systems), the transfer switch is a required part of the installation.

**Manual transfer switch:** a double-throw switch that physically disconnects the utility before connecting the generator. Cheaper and simpler; the operator must manually start and transfer.

**Automatic transfer switch (ATS):** monitors the utility supply, starts the generator, and transfers load automatically, typically within 10–30 seconds. Required for critical loads such as medical equipment and datacentre UPS bypass.

The transfer switch must be rated for the load it carries. For a 200 A main panel, a 200 A transfer switch is the common choice. For a critical-loads-only panel, a smaller switch feeds only selected circuits.

## More Q&As

### How do I account for an air conditioner that starts automatically?
An air conditioner starts on its own thermostat, so it may start while other loads are running. Enter its running watts with a start multiplier of 3–5 (check the nameplate LRA/RLA ratio). The calculator sums the running load at the moment of start and adds the extra starting kW.

### Can I run a 240 V well pump from a 120 V generator output?
No. A well pump requires 240 V (or 230 V). A generator with only 120 V outlets cannot run it. Most portable generators above 3,500 W offer a 240 V outlet; check the outlet configuration before buying.

### What does "rated power factor 0.8" mean on a generator nameplate?
It means the kVA rating is calculated at a power factor of 0.8. A 10 kVA set at 0.8 PF delivers 8 kW of real power. If the connected load has a better power factor (closer to 1.0), the same set can deliver more watts — but it cannot exceed its kVA limit.

### How much fuel does a generator use?
Roughly 0.5–0.7 L/hour per kW at half load for a diesel set, and 0.6–0.9 L/hour per kW for gasoline. At full load, consumption is 20–30 % higher. Run-time on a standard tank is usually stated on the nameplate at 50 % load, which is the standard test condition.
