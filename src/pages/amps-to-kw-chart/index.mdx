## The formulas

<div class="formula">Single-phase: P = V × I × PF / 1000<br/>Three-phase: P = √3 × V<sub>LL</sub> × I × PF / 1000</div>

P is in kW, I is the line current in amps and V<sub>LL</sub> the line-to-line voltage. The same relations in reverse are on the <a href="/kw-to-amps-chart/">kW to amps chart</a>. For a value between rows or a different voltage, use the <a href="/amps-to-watts-calculator/">amps to watts calculator</a>.

## Worked example

A 40 A single-phase circuit at 240 V carries up to 40 × 240 = 9,600 W, or 9.6 kW at a power factor of 1.0. A three-phase feeder at 480 V and 60 A with a power factor of 0.9 carries 1.732 × 480 × 60 × 0.9 = 44.9 kW, matching the 60 A row.

To change the power factor, multiply the three-phase values by PF / 0.9. At a power factor of 0.8, the 60 A row becomes 44.9 × 0.8 / 0.9 = 39.9 kW.

## Rated current is not available power

The numbers show what a given current represents, not what a circuit can safely supply. A 50 A breaker does not mean you can use 50 A for hours: continuous loads are limited to 80 % of the breaker rating under the NEC. The <a href="/breaker-amps-to-watts-chart/">breaker amps to watts chart</a> shows the usable power for each standard breaker. The conductor and its terminals also set a limit that this chart ignores.

## Questions

### How many kW is 100 amps?
At 240 V single-phase it is 24 kW. At 480 V three-phase and PF 0.9 it is 74.8 kW.

### Is kW the same as kVA?
No. kVA is apparent power, V × I with no power factor. kW is the real part, kVA × PF. A load at PF 0.9 drawing 100 kVA is delivering 90 kW. Transformers and generators are rated in kVA for that reason.

### Why does the three-phase formula have a square root of three?
The three line currents are 120° apart. Power from three balanced phases adds to √3 times the product of line voltage and line current.

### What is the kW for a 200 A, 480 V three-phase circuit at PF 0.85?
200 × 1.732 × 480 × 0.85 / 1000 = 141.0 kW.

### What current does a 50 kW three-phase load at 400 V and PF 0.9 draw?
50 × 1000 / (1.732 × 400 × 0.9) = 80.2 A.

## Common load types and their typical power factors

The chart uses a fixed power factor of 0.9 for three-phase values, which suits most motor and resistive loads. Real loads vary:

- **Resistive loads** (heaters, incandescent lighting): PF 1.0. The chart overestimates their kW by 11 %. To correct, multiply by 1.0 / 0.9 = 1.11.
- **Induction motors at full load**: PF 0.85–0.92. Close to the table assumption; use 0.9 as a starting point and refine from the nameplate.
- **Induction motors at partial load**: PF drops to 0.7–0.8. The motor draws the same current for less shaft work.
- **LED and fluorescent lighting**: PF 0.85–0.95 with an electronic ballast; can be as low as 0.5 on cheap gear without correction.
- **Variable-speed drives**: PF at the input is typically 0.95–0.98 with modern drives, but they inject harmonic currents that distort the waveform, so the apparent power meter and a true-power meter may differ.
- **Welders**: PF 0.5–0.7, highly variable with the arc cycle.

Whenever the power factor differs from 0.9, scale the three-phase table values by (actual PF / 0.9).

## Reading power from a clamp meter

A clamp meter measures current, not power. To get kW from a clamp reading:

1. Note the voltage of the circuit.
2. Note the number of phases.
3. Estimate or measure the power factor (most modern clamp meters can measure PF directly).
4. Apply the formula: single-phase P = V × I × PF / 1000; three-phase P = 1.732 × V × I × PF / 1000.

If the power factor is unknown, using 1.0 for single-phase resistive loads is accurate. For three-phase motor loads, 0.85 is a reasonable estimate at full load. The resulting kW is an approximation until PF is confirmed.

## kW versus kVA — sizing equipment correctly

Transformers and generators are rated in kVA, not kW. The difference is the power factor:

- A 100 kVA transformer at PF 0.9 supplies 90 kW of real power.
- If the same transformer supplies loads with PF 0.7, it can only deliver 70 kW before hitting its kVA limit.
- Connecting motors and electronic loads with low power factor means reaching the transformer's kVA rating before reaching the kW limit.

This is why power factor correction on large sites pays back through smaller transformer and cable sizes, not just lower utility charges.

## Effect of voltage on current for the same power

Lower supply voltage means more current for the same kW:

- 10 kW single-phase at 240 V draws 41.7 A (PF 1.0)
- 10 kW single-phase at 120 V draws 83.3 A — double the current, four times the I² R loss

This is why high-voltage distribution reduces losses over long runs, and why 480 V is preferred over 240 V for industrial motor loads. The <a href="/voltage-drop-calculator/">voltage drop calculator</a> quantifies the loss for a specific cable run.
