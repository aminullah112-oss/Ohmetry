For a balanced three-phase load, one set of readings gives all three power quantities: apparent power in kVA, real power in kW and reactive power in kvar. They form the power triangle, where kVA is the hypotenuse.

## Formulas

<div class="formula">S = √3 × V<sub>LL</sub> × I / 1000 (kVA)<br/>P = S × PF (kW)<br/>Q = S × √(1 − PF²) (kvar)</div>

V<sub>LL</sub> is the line-to-line voltage, I is the line current and PF is the power factor. The three quantities satisfy S² = P² + Q², and the calculator's tests check this identity.

## Worked example

A load draws 10 A from a 400 V three-phase supply at a power factor of 0.8.

- S = 1.732 × 400 × 10 / 1000 = 6.928 kVA
- P = 6.928 × 0.8 = 5.543 kW
- Q = 6.928 × 0.6 = 4.157 kvar

The check: 5.543² + 4.157² = 30.72 + 17.28 = 48.0, and 6.928² = 48.0.

## Where this applies

The formulas assume the three phases carry equal current and are equally loaded, with a symmetrical supply. That is a good model for motors, transformers and most industrial loads. It is not right for a panel with uneven single-phase loads, where each phase has to be calculated separately and the powers added. It also ignores harmonics, so loads dominated by drives or rectifiers may read differently on a true-RMS power meter.

The reactive power figure is what you need for power factor correction. To raise a power factor, capacitors supply some of the kvar locally. The size of the bank is the difference between the load's kvar and the kvar at the target power factor.

## How to use the calculator

Enter the line-to-line voltage, the current in each line, and the power factor. The calculator returns kVA, kW and kvar. Use a true-RMS meter reading for the current where you can, since loads with harmonics read incorrectly on average-responding meters.

## Example: sizing power factor correction

Take the earlier load of 5.543 kW at a power factor of 0.8. The reactive power is 5.543 × tan(cos⁻¹ 0.8) = 5.543 × 0.75 = 4.157 kvar. To raise the power factor to 0.95, the remaining reactive power is 5.543 × tan(cos⁻¹ 0.95) = 5.543 × 0.3287 = 1.822 kvar. The capacitor bank must supply the difference, 4.157 − 1.822 = 2.335 kvar. This is how a power factor correction capacitor bank is sized.

The line current falls too. At 0.95 the same 5.543 kW needs 5,543 / (1.732 × 400 × 0.95) = 8.42 A, down from 10 A.

## Unbalanced loads

If the three line currents differ, the single-formula result is wrong. Calculate each phase separately as V<sub>phase</sub> × I<sub>phase</sub> × PF, then add the three real powers. Do the same for reactive power, keeping the sign, since inductive and capacitive kvar offset each other.

## Typical power factors

Induction motors run at about 0.8 to 0.9 near full load, and can fall well below 0.5 at light load, which is why oversized motors waste current. Resistive heating is at 1. Fluorescent lighting with magnetic ballasts and welding equipment sit lower, while variable-speed drives and electronic loads depend on their input stage. If the meter shows both kW and kVA, divide the two to get the power factor and use the measured value instead of an assumed one.

## Questions

### Why is there a √3 in three-phase power?
Line-to-line voltage is √3 times the phase voltage in a star-connected system. Summing three phases each carrying V<sub>phase</sub> × I gives 3 × V<sub>phase</sub> × I, which equals √3 × V<sub>LL</sub> × I.

### Do I need the neutral for this calculation?
No. On a balanced load the neutral carries no current and the line-to-line formula holds.

### Delta or star connection?
The line-to-line formula is the same for both. Only the phase quantities inside the load differ.

### What power factor should I enter if I don't know it?
Use the nameplate or a meter reading. Induction motors near full load are often 0.8 to 0.9.

## Star (wye) and delta connections

The power formula uses line voltage and line current, which are defined the same way for both star and delta:

**Star (wye):** phase voltage = V_line / √3 (230 V on a 400 V system). Line current equals phase current. Used for most low-voltage distribution and small motor feeders.

**Delta:** phase voltage = V_line (400 V phase-to-phase). Line current = √3 × phase current. Used for high-current motor windings and some industrial equipment.

The formula S = √3 × V_LL × I works for both. What changes is the internal current path, not the line quantities you measure.

## Motor circuit sizing from three-phase power

For a motor, the full NEC sequence:

1. **Find the full-load current.** Use NEC Table 430.250 for the horsepower and voltage, or the nameplate. The table value governs circuit sizing. For 15 kW (20 hp) at 460 V, the table gives 27 A.
2. **Size the conductor:** 1.25 × 27 = 33.75 A minimum conductor ampacity. 10 AWG copper at 75 °C covers 35 A — acceptable. See the <a href="/wire-size-calculator/">wire size calculator</a>.
3. **Size the short-circuit protection:** for an inverse-time breaker, up to 250 % of table current for a standard motor (NEC 430.52, Table 430.52). 250 % × 27 = 67.5 A — use a 70 A breaker. This device only opens on a fault; it does not protect against running overload.
4. **Size the overload relay:** based on nameplate current (not table), typically 115–125 % of that value per NEC 430.32.
5. **Account for starting:** a direct-on-line start draws 5–8× full-load current. 27 × 6 = 162 A for a few seconds — the feeder and transformer must handle this surge without tripping.

## The power triangle

The relationship between kVA, kW and kvar forms a right triangle:

- **kVA** (hypotenuse): what the supply delivers; heats cables and determines equipment rating.
- **kW** (adjacent side): real power; converts to useful work.
- **kvar** (opposite side): reactive power; circulates between source and load doing no net work.

**Power factor correction example:** 50 kW load at PF 0.75. kVA = 50 / 0.75 = 66.7. kvar = √(66.7² − 50²) = 44.1 kvar. To reach PF 0.95: target kvar = 50 × tan(cos⁻¹ 0.95) = 50 × 0.329 = 16.4 kvar. Capacitor bank needed: 44.1 − 16.4 = 27.7 kvar. The <a href="/power-factor-correction-calculator/">power factor correction calculator</a> does this directly.

## More Q&As

### How do I calculate three-phase kVA from a clamp meter?
Clamp one line and measure current. Measure the voltage between any two phases (line-to-line). Then S = √3 × V_LL × I / 1,000. For example, 25 A on a 415 V system: S = 1.732 × 415 × 25 / 1,000 = 17.97 kVA.

### What is the difference between kVA and kW on a three-phase system?
kVA is apparent power — what flows through the cables. kW is real power — what gets converted to useful work. The ratio kW/kVA is the power factor. A lower power factor means more current in the cables for the same useful output.

### How many amps is 100 kVA at 400 V three-phase?
I = 100,000 / (1.732 × 400) = 144.3 A. This is the full-load current, the value used to size conductors and overcurrent devices on the secondary of a 100 kVA transformer.

### Can I use the three-phase formula for a single-phase load connected to one phase?
No. A single-phase load on one phase of a three-phase system uses only one phase and neutral (or one phase-to-phase pair for delta). Use P = V × I × PF with the phase voltage or phase-to-phase voltage.
