Motors are rated in kilowatts in most of the world and in horsepower in North America. Both measure the mechanical power at the shaft, so the conversion is a fixed ratio. What is not fixed is which horsepower you mean, and how much electrical power the motor draws to deliver it.

## Formulas

<div class="formula">hp = kW × 1000 / 745.7 (mechanical)<br/>kW = hp × 745.7 / 1000<br/>Input kW = shaft kW / efficiency</div>

One mechanical (imperial) horsepower is 745.7 W, so 1 kW is 1.341 hp. Metric horsepower, written PS or CV, is 735.5 W, so 1 kW is 1.360 PS. The difference is about 1.4 %, which matters when you read a European nameplate.

## Worked example

A 7.5 kW motor is 7,500 / 745.7 = 10.06 hp, usually called a 10 hp motor.

A 10 hp motor delivers 10 × 745.7 = 7,457 W at the shaft. If its efficiency is 90 %, the electrical input is 7.457 / 0.9 = 8.29 kW.

## From horsepower to amps

Horsepower rates the shaft, but the supply carries the electrical input, and a motor also has losses and a power factor. For sizing under the NEC, do not calculate the current from horsepower. Use the full-load current in Table 430.250, which the <a href="/motor-circuit-calculator/">motor circuit calculator</a> reads for you. A 10 hp three-phase motor on 460 V has a table full-load current of 14 A. For a measured or nameplate load, use the <a href="/kw-to-amps-calculator/">kW to amps calculator</a> with the input power and the power factor.

## Common mistakes

- **Using shaft power as the electrical input.** The motor draws more than it delivers. A 90 % efficient motor draws 11 % more than its shaft output.
- **Mixing mechanical and metric horsepower.** A "10 hp" label in Europe is often 10 PS, which is 7.35 kW, not 7.46 kW.
- **Using horsepower for the circuit.** The NEC sizes motor conductors and protection from table current, not from a calculated value.
- **Forgetting that kW on an IEC nameplate is the shaft output.** The input is higher by the efficiency.

## Questions

### How many kW is 1 hp?
0.7457 kW for mechanical horsepower, or 0.7355 kW for metric horsepower.

### How many hp is 1 kW?
1.341 mechanical hp, or 1.360 metric horsepower.

### How many amps is a 10 hp motor?
It depends on voltage and phase. On a three-phase 460 V supply the NEC table value is 14 A, and on 230 V it is 28 A.

### Is a 7.5 kW motor a 10 hp motor?
Yes, within about 0.6 %. 7.5 kW is 10.06 hp.

### How do variable-speed drives affect the kW and hp relationship?
The drive controls the motor's speed and therefore its shaft output. The motor nameplate kW is the maximum. At reduced speed the torque drops, so the shaft kW is lower — and the input kW drops too. The drive's input kW depends on the motor shaft load and the drive's own efficiency (typically 95–98 %). For energy calculations at partial speed, the kW relationship still holds: shaft kW / efficiency = input kW.

### Why do IEC nameplates list kW but NEMA nameplates list hp?
Historical convention. The USA standardised on horsepower early in industrial history and the NEMA motor standards followed. The rest of the world moved to SI units, so IEC motors show kW directly. Neither is wrong — they are the same quantity, different units.

## Mechanical and metric horsepower — where the difference matters

The 1.4 % gap between mechanical hp (745.7 W) and metric PS (735.5 W) is usually ignored in practice, but it matters when:

- An imported European motor shows "10 PS" and the cable was sized for a 10 hp (North American) motor. The motor actually draws slightly less, so this is conservative and harmless.
- A pump curve from a European manufacturer shows shaft power in PS but the motor catalog shows input current for hp ratings. Matching them exactly requires the conversion.
- A generator or transformer is sized from a motor hp rating on the datasheet, and the datasheet is from a European supplier listing PS. The difference is small, but it adds to the safety margin either way.

## Efficiency at partial load

A motor's nameplate efficiency is at full load. At partial load, efficiency typically drops:

| Load % | Typical IE3 efficiency |
|---|---|
| 100 % | 93–96 % |
| 75 % | 93–96 % (often close to full-load) |
| 50 % | 90–94 % |
| 25 % | 80–87 % |

This means a motor running at 25 % load draws proportionally more electrical kW per shaft kW than at full load. For energy cost calculations, using full-load efficiency as a constant underestimates the electricity consumption of lightly loaded motors.

Variable-speed drives allow motors to run at the speed that matches the load, which keeps them in a higher-efficiency band. This is the main energy argument for drives on pump and fan applications with variable flow.

## Sizing a circuit from kW

When you have the shaft kW and motor efficiency, the electrical input kW follows. To get the current for circuit sizing under NEC Article 430:

1. Find the shaft hp: hp = kW × 1.341
2. Look up the full-load current in NEC Table 430.250 (three-phase) or Table 430.248 (single-phase) for the appropriate voltage.
3. Apply 125 % for the branch circuit conductor (continuous motor load).
4. Size the overcurrent protection to the values in NEC Table 430.52.

The <a href="/motor-circuit-calculator/">motor circuit calculator</a> does all of this in one step from the hp and voltage.

Do not substitute a calculated current for the NEC table value — the table values include diversity and starting considerations that a simple kW / V formula does not.

## kW in generator and transformer specifications

A generator is rated in kVA and in kW at a stated power factor (typically 0.8 lagging). To find whether a generator can carry a motor load:

1. Convert shaft hp to electrical input kW: input kW = (hp × 0.7457) / efficiency
2. Find the kVA: kVA = input kW / power factor
3. Add other connected loads and apply a demand factor
4. Add a surge allowance for starting — motors can draw 6–10× full-load kVA for the first few cycles

A generator sized only on running kW may fail to start the motors. The <a href="/generator-size-calculator/">generator size calculator</a> handles motor starting surge.
