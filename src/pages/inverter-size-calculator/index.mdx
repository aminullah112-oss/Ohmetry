An inverter has two ratings that matter: how much it can supply continuously and how much it can supply for a few seconds. Most undersized inverters fail on the second one, when a pump or compressor starts and trips the unit.

## Formulas

<div class="formula">Continuous (W) = total running load × (1 + margin)<br/>Surge (W) = running load − motor running W + motor starting W<br/>DC current (A) = continuous W / (battery V × efficiency)</div>

The margin defaults to 25 %, so the inverter doesn't run at its limit all day and has room for loads added later. The surge figure assumes one large motor starts while everything else is already running, which is the usual worst case. Enter the largest motor's running and starting watts, or zero if there is none.

## Worked example

A workshop has 3,000 W of loads on at once, including a 800 W pump that draws 2,400 W when it starts, on a 48 V battery.

- Continuous: 3,000 × 1.25 = 3,750 W, so a 4 kW inverter is the nearest standard size up.
- Surge: 3,000 − 800 + 2,400 = 4,600 W. The inverter's surge rating, and how long it can hold it, must exceed this.
- DC current: 3,750 / (48 × 0.9) = 86.8 A from the battery at full load.

## Motor starting current

Induction motors draw several times their running current for a fraction of a second, a figure often given as locked-rotor current. Multipliers of 3 to 7 are common, and loaded compressors and well pumps sit at the high end. Read the starting watts or locked-rotor amps from the nameplate or the datasheet when you can. Soft starters and variable-frequency drives cut the starting surge a good deal.

## Why battery voltage matters here

The DC current rises as battery voltage falls. The same 3,750 W load draws about 87 A from a 48 V bank but about 347 A from a 12 V bank, which needs very heavy cable and high-current fuses. That is the reason larger inverters are built for 24 V and 48 V.

## Choosing an inverter

- **Waveform.** A pure sine wave inverter suits motors, electronics and anything sensitive. Modified sine wave units can cause heating or noise in some loads.
- **Surge duration.** Datasheets state a surge rating for a stated time, often a few seconds. Check that the start time of your motor fits inside it.
- **Efficiency.** Conversion efficiency is lower at light load, and the standby draw adds up for a system that runs all day.
- **Derating.** Ratings often assume moderate temperatures. In a hot enclosure the usable output can be lower.

## Common mistakes

- **Adding every appliance together.** Only the loads that can run at the same time count, so a realistic duty pattern gives a smaller inverter than the sum of the nameplates.
- **Ignoring motor starting.**
- **Matching inverter size to the panels.** The array and the inverter are sized independently in off-grid systems.

## Questions

### What size inverter do I need to run a fridge?
A fridge compressor may run at 100 to 150 W but start at several times that. Look at the nameplate or measure it, and check the surge rating.

### Can I run a 3 kW load on a 3 kW inverter?
Only without a margin, and without any surge. Choose a larger inverter or reduce the load.

### Does the inverter size depend on the battery?
The battery must be able to deliver the DC current, and the battery voltage must match the inverter's input.

## Inverter size reference table

Minimum continuous inverter rating for common off-grid and backup applications:

| Application | Running load | Motor start peak | Minimum inverter |
|---|---|---|---|
| Small cabin (lights, laptop, fan) | 300 W | 300 W | 500 W |
| Cabin with water pump (1/2 hp) | 800 W | 2,100 W | 2,500 W |
| Home essentials (fridge, lights, outlets) | 1,500 W | 3,500 W | 4,000 W |
| Small workshop (tools + lighting) | 3,000 W | 6,000 W | 6,000 W |
| Home with AC (1.5-ton) | 2,500 W | 7,000 W | 8,000 W |

Starting peak = running load − motor running W + motor locked-rotor W. The inverter's surge rating must exceed the peak for the time the motor takes to start, typically 2–5 seconds.

## Worked example: 48 V home backup system

A home wants to run 1,200 W of lighting and outlets, a 150 W fridge (450 W start, multiplier 3), and a 750 W well pump (2,250 W start) on a 48 V lithium bank.

**Running load:** 1,200 + 150 + 750 = 2,100 W.

**Continuous rating (25 % margin):** 2,100 × 1.25 = 2,625 W. Next standard size: **3,000 W**.

**Starting surge (pump starts while everything else runs):**
2,100 − 750 + 2,250 = 3,600 W. The inverter's 3-second surge rating must exceed 3,600 W.

**DC current at full load:** 2,625 / (48 × 0.9) = 60.8 A. Cable from battery to inverter must handle 61 A continuous, so 6 AWG copper at 60 °C is marginal — use 4 AWG.

**Battery fuse:** rated just above 61 A continuous, with an interrupting rating adequate for the battery's short-circuit current (typically 300–500 A for lithium packs at 48 V).

## Waveform and load compatibility

| Load type | Modified sine wave | Pure sine wave |
|---|---|---|
| Resistive (heaters, incandescent bulbs) | Works | Works |
| LED lighting | Usually works | Works |
| Induction motors (pumps, fans) | May run hot, reduced life | Works |
| Variable-speed drives | Avoid | Works |
| Sensitive electronics (medical, audio) | Avoid | Works |
| Battery chargers | Often works | Works |

For any system with motors, variable-speed drives, or medical equipment, specify a pure sine wave inverter. The cost premium over modified sine wave units is typically 15–30 % and is worth it for the load compatibility.

## DC cable sizing from battery to inverter

The battery-to-inverter cable is the highest-current run in the system. At 48 V:

| Inverter rating | DC current (@ 90% eff) | Minimum cable (copper) |
|---|---|---|
| 1,000 W | 23 A | 10 AWG |
| 2,000 W | 46 A | 6 AWG |
| 3,000 W | 69 A | 4 AWG |
| 5,000 W | 116 A | 1 AWG |
| 8,000 W | 185 A | 3/0 AWG |

Keep cable runs short — every metre adds resistance and voltage drop. Under 1 m is ideal for the battery-inverter link; keep total voltage drop below 0.5 V at full load.

## More Q&As

### What is the difference between rated and surge power on an inverter?
Rated (continuous) power is what the inverter can supply indefinitely. Surge power is what it can supply for a few seconds (typically 2–5 s) during motor start. Always check both: a 3,000 W inverter may have a 6,000 W surge rating, which is what lets it start a 1 hp pump.

### Why does efficiency drop at light load?
Inverters draw a constant standby current regardless of load. At very light loads this standby power is a large fraction of output, so efficiency falls. A 3,000 W inverter running a 100 W load may convert at 60–70 % rather than the 90–95 % quoted at full load.

### Can I connect two inverters in parallel to get more power?
Only if the inverters are specifically designed and listed for parallel operation. Standard inverters cannot be paralleled — the phase mismatch will damage both units.

### How do I know if my inverter is big enough for an air conditioner?
Take the AC unit's starting kW (usually 2.5–3× its rated kW) and check it against the inverter's surge rating. A 1.5 ton (5.3 kW thermal) AC may need 7–9 kW of surge capacity for the first 2 seconds of compressor start.
