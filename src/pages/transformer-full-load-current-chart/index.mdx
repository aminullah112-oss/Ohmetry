The full-load current of a transformer is the current at its rated kVA. It sets the minimum size of conductors and overcurrent devices on each side. The tables cover standard three-phase sizes at the usual secondary voltages and at common primary voltages.

## The formula

<div class="formula">I = kVA × 1000 / (√3 × V<sub>LL</sub>)</div>

kVA is the rated apparent power and V<sub>LL</sub> the line-to-line voltage on the side you are checking. Full-load current is independent of the load power factor, because kVA already includes it. The <a href="/transformer-full-load-current-calculator/">transformer full-load current calculator</a> gives both sides at once for any rating. For a single-phase unit, divide kVA × 1000 by the voltage.

## Worked example

A 500 kVA transformer with a 480 V secondary has a full-load current of 500,000 / (1.732 × 480) = 601 A, matching the chart. The same transformer on a 208 V secondary has 1,388 A. The lower the voltage, the larger the bus, cable and breaker needed for the same kVA.

On a 13,800 V primary the same 500 kVA unit draws 20.9 A, so the primary side is a small cable and a modest fuse. This large ratio between sides is also why the primary and secondary protection are chosen separately.

## What the number is used for

- **Conductor and device sizing.** NEC Article 450 sets the maximum overcurrent protection as a percentage of the full-load current, and the percentage depends on the voltage and on whether protection is on one or both sides. Check the current edition for the exact figures.
- **Inrush.** On energizing, a transformer can draw many times its full-load current for a short time. Protective devices must ride through it.
- **Fault level.** The fault current at the secondary follows from the full-load current and the impedance. See the <a href="/transformer-short-circuit-current-chart/">transformer fault current chart</a>.

## Questions

### What is the full-load current of a 1,000 kVA transformer at 480 V?
1,203 A on the secondary.

### What is the full-load current of a 75 kVA transformer at 208 V?
208 A.

### Do the primary and secondary currents multiply to the same kVA?
Yes. 500 kVA at 13,800 V gives 20.9 A, and the same 500 kVA at 480 V gives 601 A. The power is equal on both sides, ignoring the small losses.

### What is the full-load current of a 2,500 kVA transformer at 480 V?
2,500,000 / (1.732 × 480) = 3,007 A.

### What is the full-load current on the 11 kV primary of a 1,000 kVA transformer?
1,000,000 / (1.732 × 11,000) = 52.5 A.

## Sizing conductors and overcurrent protection from the chart

The full-load current on each side of the transformer sets the starting point for cable and device sizing, but the final size follows from the applicable standard.

**NEC Article 450 — overcurrent protection**

For transformers rated over 600 V, Article 450.3 sets the maximum overcurrent protection as a percentage of the rated current. The table values depend on whether protection is on the primary side, the secondary side, or both. Common limits:

- Primary protection only, 600 V and below: 125 % of primary full-load current for fuses; 167 % for circuit breakers if the calculated value does not correspond to a standard size.
- Both primary and secondary protection: primary 250 %, secondary 125 %.
- Supervised installations (industrial) have different limits.

Always verify against the current edition of the NEC for the exact row that applies to your voltage and situation.

**Conductor sizing**

A transformer secondary conductor is not a motor branch circuit. It does not get the automatic 125 % factor unless it feeds a continuous load. The conductor must be rated for the full-load current on that side. For a 500 kVA transformer at 480 V, the secondary full-load current is 601 A, which requires 1,000 kcmil copper or three sets of 250 kcmil in parallel, depending on conduit fill and ambient temperature.

## Inrush and its effect on protection

On energising, the transformer draws magnetising inrush current. For distribution transformers this is typically 8–12 times the full-load current for the first few cycles, decaying over several seconds. The exact multiple depends on:

- The design and core material
- The residual flux in the core when last switched off
- The point on the voltage wave at energisation

An 8× inrush on a 601 A transformer is 4,808 A for a fraction of a second. Fuses protecting the transformer must not blow on this. Standard transformer fuses (K-type or T-type current-limiting fuses) are selected with this in mind from the manufacturer's curve or table. Relays use an inrush-restraint element or a time delay set to ride through the inrush period.

## Taps and voltage adjustment

Distribution transformers often have no-load taps (±2.5 % and ±5 %) that allow the secondary voltage to be trimmed. If a transformer is set to its +5 % tap:

- The secondary voltage rises by 5 %
- The turn ratio changes, so the secondary full-load current changes by approximately the same factor
- A transformer on the +5 % tap at 480 V delivers about 504 V. The full-load current decreases to maintain the same kVA.

Transformer tap adjustments must be made with the transformer de-energised. Only load tap changers (LTCs), found on substation power transformers, allow on-load adjustment.

## Single-phase transformers

Single-phase transformers use the formula I = kVA × 1000 / V, with no √3 factor. A 25 kVA single-phase unit at 240 V has a full-load current of 25,000 / 240 = 104.2 A. The <a href="/kva-to-amps-calculator/">kVA to amps calculator</a> handles both phases.
