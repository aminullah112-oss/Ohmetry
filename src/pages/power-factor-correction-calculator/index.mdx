Most industrial loads are inductive: motors, transformers and lighting ballasts draw reactive power as well as real power. The extra current heats cables, takes capacity from transformers and, on many tariffs, costs money. A capacitor bank supplies the reactive power locally and lowers the current drawn from the supply. This calculator finds the size of the bank.

## Formula

<div class="formula">Q<sub>c</sub> = P × (tan φ<sub>1</sub> − tan φ<sub>2</sub>)<br/>φ = cos⁻¹(power factor)</div>

P is the real power in kW, φ<sub>1</sub> the angle at the present power factor and φ<sub>2</sub> the angle at the target. Q<sub>c</sub> comes out in kvar. The real power does not change, so the apparent power after correction is simply P divided by the new power factor.

## Worked example

A plant draws 100 kW at a power factor of 0.80 and the target is 0.95.

- tan(cos⁻¹ 0.80) = 0.750 and tan(cos⁻¹ 0.95) = 0.329.
- Q<sub>c</sub> = 100 × (0.750 − 0.329) = 42.1 kvar.
- Apparent power falls from 125 kVA to 105.3 kVA, and the line current falls by 15.8 %.

Correcting to unity would take 75 kvar, which is the full reactive power of the load. Going from 0.95 to unity costs a lot of extra capacitance for a small further gain, which is why targets of 0.95 to 0.98 are common.

## How a bank is built

Banks are built from stock capacitor sizes, so the result is rounded up to the next available step, or split into steps of a controller-switched bank when the load varies. A fixed bank sized for the maximum demand overcorrects at light load. A power factor controller, switching steps in and out, tracks the load. For a single motor, the capacitor can be connected at the motor terminals, but the size must not exceed the limit the motor manufacturer gives, otherwise a self-excited overvoltage can occur when the motor is switched off while the capacitor stays connected.

## Harmonics are the main trap

Capacitors and the supply inductance form a resonant circuit. If the resonant frequency sits near a harmonic produced by drives, rectifiers or other non-linear loads, the capacitors can draw high currents and fail, and voltage distortion rises. Where such loads are a large part of the demand, the bank is fitted with series detuning reactors, which move the resonance below the lowest significant harmonic, and the harmonic spectrum is measured before the bank is chosen. This calculation assumes a sinusoidal load.

## What else to check

- **Voltage rise.** A capacitor bank lifts the voltage slightly at its point of connection, more on weak supplies.
- **Switching.** Capacitor contactors carry high inrush currents and are specified for the duty.
- **Overcorrection.** A load that goes leading is penalised by some utilities, and can cause overvoltage.
- **Protection.** Fuses and breakers are rated for the capacitor current, which is higher than the nominal because of tolerance and harmonics. Follow the capacitor manufacturer's selection rules.

## Common mistakes

- **Using kVA instead of kW.** The formula needs real power.
- **Using the nameplate power factor, not the measured one.** Take readings at typical load, which are often lower than the nameplate.
- **Sizing for peak demand with a fixed bank.**

## Questions

### What power factor should I target?
Utility tariffs and rules differ. A target of 0.95 is common, but check the tariff, which may set a penalty threshold.

### Why does correction reduce the line current but not the energy used?
The kW stay the same. Only the reactive component, which does no work, is removed from the supply side.

### Can I correct at the main incomer instead of at each motor?
Yes, and it is cheaper. It lowers the current in the supply and the transformer, but not in the cables between the incomer and each motor.

## Worked example: manufacturing plant

A plant runs 350 kW of motor load at a measured power factor of 0.72. The utility tariff applies a penalty below PF 0.90, and the site wants to reach PF 0.95 for headroom.

**Step 1 — calculate kvar needed:**
tan φ₁ = tan(arccos 0.72) = 0.964
tan φ₂ = tan(arccos 0.95) = 0.329
Qc = 350 × (0.964 − 0.329) = 350 × 0.635 = **222 kvar**

**Step 2 — check apparent power reduction:**
Before: S = 350 / 0.72 = 486 kVA. After: S = 350 / 0.95 = 368 kVA.
Reduction: 118 kVA. At a demand tariff of £8/kVA/month, annual saving = 118 × 8 × 12 = **£11,328/year**.

**Step 3 — select bank:**
Specify a 225 kvar bank in 3 × 75 kvar automatic steps. Allow at minimum 7% detuning filters if significant harmonic content is present.

## Capacitor bank hardware

| Component | Purpose | Sizing rule |
|---|---|---|
| Power capacitors | Supply reactive current | kvar × 1.1 (10% overrating for voltage tolerance) |
| Detuning reactor | Prevent harmonic resonance | 7% detuned reactor for 5th harmonic sites |
| Contactor | Switch steps | Rated for capacitive switching (AC-6b category) |
| Fuses | Protect capacitors | Current-limiting HRC, 1.65 × capacitor rated current |
| Controller | Auto-step selection | Reactive power relay, target PF setpoint |

Standard capacitor steps are 5, 10, 25, 50 or 100 kvar. A 7% detuned reactor shifts the resonant frequency below the 5th harmonic (250 Hz on 50 Hz systems) and is the standard choice for sites with 6-pulse VFDs.

## Harmonic distortion and de-tuned banks

A plain capacitor bank resonates with the supply inductance at a frequency that may coincide with a harmonic. For a site with variable-speed drives or other rectifier loads, measure total harmonic voltage distortion (THDv) first. If THDv > 5%, specify a de-tuned (series reactor) or harmonic filter bank:

- **7% detuned**: resonant frequency ~190 Hz — avoids 5th harmonic resonance
- **14% detuned**: resonant frequency ~134 Hz — avoids both 3rd and 5th

A de-tuned bank absorbs slightly fewer vars than a plain bank of the same rating (the reactor consumes some kvar). Factor in a 7–14% upsize.

## More Q&As

### What is the payback period for a power factor correction bank?
Typically 1–3 years for industrial sites billed on kVA demand tariffs. The saving comes from reduced apparent power demand charges and reduced cable losses. Calculate P×(1/PF₁ − 1/PF₂) × demand charge rate × 12 months for the annual saving.

### Where should the capacitor bank be installed?
As close to the inductive load as practical. Central MV bus correction lowers upstream cable losses and utility demand charges. Load-level correction (a capacitor on each motor terminal) eliminates reactive current on the entire distribution network. For large sites, a mix is common: a central automatic bank for base load, with fixed capacitors at large motors.

### Can I over-correct power factor?
Yes. If the bank is too large, the site supplies reactive power to the grid (leading power factor). Most utilities permit a small leading PF (e.g. 0.95 leading), but during light-load periods the capacitors must be switched off in steps. An automatic controller with a reactive power relay handles this.
