**Short answer:** kW = kVA × power factor. At a power factor of 0.8, 1 kVA is 0.8 kW and 100 kVA is 80 kW. At 0.9, 100 kVA is 90 kW, and at a power factor of 1 the two are equal. The tables below cover common kVA ratings and power factors.

## The formulas

<div class="formula">kW = kVA × PF<br/>kVA = kW / PF<br/>kvar = √(kVA² − kW²)</div>

kVA is apparent power, the total the supply has to carry. kW is real power, the part that does useful work. The power factor (PF) is the ratio between them and is between 0 and 1. Use the <a href="/kva-to-kw-calculator/">kVA to kW calculator</a> for any value that is not in the table, and it also gives the reactive kvar.

## Single-phase and three-phase

The conversion is the same. kVA already includes the voltage, the current and, for three-phase, the √3, so kW = kVA × PF holds for both. Only the current changes.

For example, a three-phase load drawing 100 A at 480 V is √3 × 480 × 100 / 1,000 = 83.1 kVA. At a power factor of 0.85 that is 83.1 × 0.85 = 70.7 kW. The <a href="/kva-to-amps-calculator/">kVA to amps calculator</a> and the <a href="/transformer-full-load-current-chart/">transformer full-load current chart</a> give the current for a given kVA.

## Worked examples

- **A 500 kVA generator at 0.8 power factor** delivers 500 × 0.8 = 400 kW. Look at the 500 kVA row, PF 0.8 column.
- **A 250 kW load at 0.9 power factor** needs 250 / 0.9 = 277.8 kVA. The nearest standard kVA rating above that is the one to choose, so the <a href="/generator-size-calculator/">generator size calculator</a> is the next step if you are sizing a set.
- **A 100 kVA transformer feeding motors at 0.85** can supply 100 × 0.85 = 85 kW of real load before it reaches its kVA limit.

## Which power factor to use

- Resistive heaters and incandescent lamps: 1.0.
- Induction motors at full load: 0.8 to 0.9, and much lower when lightly loaded.
- LED drivers and switch-mode supplies: often 0.9 or better, but check the label.
- Generators are commonly rated at 0.8, so a 100 kVA set delivers 80 kW. Modern UPS units are often rated at 0.9 or 1.0.
- A site with a mix of loads has a combined power factor. Use the measured figure if you have it.

## Common mistakes

- **Reading kW as the rating of a kVA machine.** A 100 kVA generator is not a 100 kW generator unless the power factor is 1.
- **Sizing a transformer or generator from kW alone.** Divide by the power factor first.
- **Using a power factor above 1.** It cannot exceed 1.
- **Adding kW and kVA values together.** Add kW to kW, and add kVA only as vectors with the reactive power, not as plain numbers.

## Questions

### How many kW is 1 kVA?
At a power factor of 0.8, 1 kVA is 0.8 kW. At 0.9 it is 0.9 kW, and at 1.0 it is 1 kW.

### How many kW is 100 kVA?
At 0.8 it is 80 kW, at 0.9 it is 90 kW and at 1.0 it is 100 kW.

### Is the kVA to kW formula different for three-phase?
No. kW = kVA × PF for single-phase and three-phase. The three-phase factor of √3 is already inside the kVA value.

### Why is the answer smaller than the kVA?
Because the power factor is below 1. The difference is reactive power, in kvar, which does no useful work but still loads the cables and the supply.
