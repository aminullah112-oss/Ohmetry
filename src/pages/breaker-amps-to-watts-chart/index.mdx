A breaker rating in amps sets how much load the circuit can carry. This chart converts standard breaker ratings into watts at the common voltages. The values are the largest continuous load, 80 % of the breaker rating, because a continuous load is the case that usually governs. The single-phase columns use a power factor of 1.0, and the three-phase columns multiply by √3.

## Why 80 %

Under the NEC, the overcurrent device for a continuous load, one expected to run for three hours or more, must be rated at least 125 % of that load (210.20(A) for branch circuits). Turned around, a breaker supports a continuous load of 1 / 1.25 = 80 % of its rating. A noncontinuous load can use the full rating, up to the limit of the wire.

<div class="formula">Continuous load (W) = 0.8 × breaker amps × V (single-phase)<br/>Continuous load (W) = 0.8 × breaker amps × √3 × V<sub>LL</sub> (three-phase)</div>

## Worked example

A 20 A, 120 V circuit supports a continuous load of 0.8 × 20 × 120 = 1,920 W, which is the figure in the table. At its full rating, 20 × 120 = 2,400 W is available for loads that are not continuous. A 50 A, 240 V circuit supports 9,600 W continuously, a figure used for sizing an EV charger circuit: a 40 A charger on a 50 A breaker is exactly at the limit.

To go the other way, a continuous 3,000 W load at 240 V draws 12.5 A. The breaker must be at least 1.25 × 12.5 = 15.6 A, so choose a 20 A breaker. The <a href="/breaker-size-calculator/">breaker size calculator</a> does this for any load.

## What the chart does not tell you

- **The wire sets a second limit.** A 20 A breaker needs a conductor rated for 20 A or more. The <a href="/wire-size-calculator/">wire size calculator</a> checks the conductor.
- **The panel and the supply.** The sum of all circuits is limited by the panel rating and the service, not by adding up breaker sizes.
- **Specific loads have their own rules.** Motors, welders, air-conditioning equipment and EV chargers each have their own sections in the NEC.
- **Power factor.** For a lagging load, real power is lower than shown.

## Questions

### How many watts can a 15 amp breaker handle?
1,800 W at 120 V at its full rating, and 1,440 W continuously.

### How many watts is a 100 amp panel?
At 240 V single-phase, 24,000 W at the full rating of the main breaker. Real service sizing uses a load calculation, not the rating of the main.

### Can a 20 amp breaker run a 2,000 W heater?
At 120 V the heater draws 16.7 A. If it runs for three hours or more it is a continuous load and 16.7 A exceeds 80 % of 20 A (16 A), so use a 240 V heater or a larger circuit.

### What is the difference between a tandem breaker and a standard breaker?
A tandem (slim or half-size) breaker occupies one slot but provides two circuits, each with its own trip. It is used to add a circuit to a full panel without a full-size slot. The combined current is not doubled — each pole is independent, typically 15 or 20 A. Not all panels accept tandem breakers, and the panel's listing labels say which slots allow them.

### Do GFCI and AFCI breakers change the watt ratings?
No. The continuous load limit (80 % of rating) is the same. GFCI protection trips faster on ground faults; AFCI protection responds to arc signatures. Neither changes the thermal or magnetic trip current.

## NEC continuous load rule in practice

NEC 210.20(A) applies to all branch circuits, not only 120 V circuits. Some examples:

| Circuit | Breaker | 80 % continuous |
|---|---|---|
| 120 V / 20 A lighting | 20 A | 1,920 W |
| 240 V / 50 A range | 50 A | 9,600 W |
| 120/240 V / 60 A dryer | 60 A | 11,520 W |
| 480 V three-phase / 30 A | 30 A | 19,948 W |
| 480 V three-phase / 100 A | 100 A | 66,494 W |

The 80 % applies to the breaker rating, not to the wire ampacity. If the wire's ampacity is the binding constraint, that limit governs instead.

## GFCI and AFCI requirements by circuit type

NEC 2023 requires GFCI protection on all 120 V through 250 V receptacle outlets in many locations, and AFCI protection on most bedroom and living area circuits.

**GFCI required (not exhaustive):** bathrooms, garages, outdoors, crawl spaces, basements, boat hoists, boat houses, kitchen countertop surfaces, rooftop, swimming pool areas.

**AFCI required (not exhaustive):** all 120 V, 15 and 20 A branch circuits supplying outlets in dwelling unit bedrooms, family rooms, dining rooms, living rooms, parlors, libraries, dens, sunrooms, recreation rooms, closets, hallways, laundry areas, similar rooms.

**Combination AFCI/GFCI breakers** cover both requirements in one device. For new construction these are increasingly common for covered areas.

AFCI and GFCI protection can be provided at the first outlet in a circuit rather than the breaker, but breaker-level protection covers the entire run, including the cable from the panel.

## Panel load calculation

Adding a new circuit requires checking that the panel and service are not overloaded. The standard method (NEC 220.40) for a dwelling unit:

1. **General lighting and receptacles**: 3 VA per square foot of living area
2. **Small appliance circuits**: 1,500 VA each (minimum two for kitchen)
3. **Laundry circuit**: 1,500 VA
4. **Major appliances**: nameplate rating (range, dryer, HVAC, water heater)
5. **Apply demand factors**: first 10,000 VA at 100 %, remainder at 40 %

The result gives the minimum service size in amperes at 240 V. A typical 2,000 ft² home works out to around 100–125 A. A 200 A service provides margin for EV charging, heat pumps and future loads.

## 240 V versus 120 V circuits

The same real power requires twice the current on a 120 V circuit as on a 240 V circuit. A 2,400 W load:
- At 120 V: 20 A — needs a 25 A breaker for continuous load, or a 20 A breaker if the load draws 20 A for under 3 hours
- At 240 V: 10 A — fits a 15 A breaker with room to spare

For fixed appliances such as water heaters, dryers and EV chargers, 240 V is preferred because it halves the current, halves the I²R losses, and allows a smaller conductor.

## NEC Table 210.21 — receptacle ratings

NEC Table 210.21(B)(3) limits the receptacle rating to the circuit rating:

| Circuit ampere rating | Receptacle rating |
|---|---|
| 15 A | 15 A |
| 20 A | 15 A or 20 A |
| 30 A | 30 A |
| 40 A | 40 or 50 A |
| 50 A | 50 A |

A 20 A circuit may use 15 A receptacles — the circuit can supply multiple 15 A outlets without overloading them, because the breaker protects the total. Only one 15 A appliance plugs in at a time per outlet. A 30 A circuit needs a 30 A receptacle; a standard 15 A plug will not fit, which prevents inadvertent overloading.
