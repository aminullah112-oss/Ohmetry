Horsepower to kilowatts is a unit conversion: one mechanical horsepower is 745.7 W. This chart gives the shaft power in kW for common motor sizes, the same value in metric horsepower, and the electrical input power the motor draws at 90 % efficiency.

## The formulas

<div class="formula">kW = HP × 0.7457 (mechanical, 745.7 W)<br/>kW = HP × 0.7355 (metric, PS or CV, 735.5 W)<br/>Input kW = shaft kW / efficiency</div>

Mechanical horsepower is the unit used on North American motor nameplates. Metric horsepower is common in Europe and on imported equipment, and it is about 1.4 % smaller. The <a href="/kw-to-hp-calculator/">kW to HP calculator</a> converts any value in either direction.

## Worked example

A 30 HP motor delivers 30 × 0.7457 = 22.4 kW at the shaft. At an efficiency of 90 % it draws 22.37 / 0.9 = 24.9 kW from the supply, as the 30 HP row shows. In a 480 V three-phase circuit at a power factor of 0.85, that is a running current of about 35 A, which you can find from the <a href="/kw-to-amps-chart/">kW to amps chart</a> scaled for the power factor.

In the other direction, a 50 kW motor is 50 / 0.7457 = 67.1 HP, so the next standard size up is a 75 HP motor.

## Shaft power is not electrical power

A motor's HP or kW rating is its mechanical output. The electrical input is larger by the losses. Small motors have efficiencies of 80 to 85 %, large ones above 95 %. Use the efficiency from the nameplate or data sheet when you need the real electrical load, such as for a generator or a transformer.

## Do not size the circuit from this chart

The NEC does not size motor branch circuits from the kW or from a running-current calculation. It uses table full-load current values by horsepower and voltage, which differ from the real current, and it adds its own factors for overload and short-circuit protection. For the circuit, use the <a href="/hp-to-amps-calculator/">HP to amps calculator</a> and the <a href="/motor-circuit-calculator/">motor circuit calculator</a>.

## Questions

### How many kW is a 10 HP motor?
7.457 kW of shaft power, and about 8.3 kW of electrical input at 90 % efficiency.

### Is 1 HP the same as 746 W?
Mechanical horsepower is 745.7 W, which rounds to 746 W. Metric horsepower is 735.5 W.

### How many HP is 1 kW?
1.341 mechanical HP.

### What is the kW equivalent of a 5 hp motor?
5 × 0.7457 = 3.73 kW at the shaft. At 90 % efficiency the electrical input is 4.14 kW.

### How do I find the shaft kW from the nameplate?
IEC nameplates list shaft kW directly. NEMA nameplates list HP; multiply by 0.7457. The input kW is the shaft kW divided by the efficiency, which the nameplate also gives as a percentage.

## Motor efficiency classes

Efficiency at the shaft matters for the electrical load, running cost and generator or transformer size. Modern standards define minimum efficiency levels by class:

**IEC 60034-30-1 (European and international)**

| Class | Description |
|---|---|
| IE1 | Standard efficiency (mostly discontinued for new sales) |
| IE2 | High efficiency — the minimum for most applications in many markets |
| IE3 | Premium efficiency — mandatory in the EU above 0.75 kW for most uses since 2015 |
| IE4 | Super premium efficiency — required in some jurisdictions above 75 kW |

**NEMA (North American)**

NEMA Premium is the equivalent of IE3. NEMA High Efficiency is roughly IE2.

For a rough check, typical IE3/NEMA Premium efficiencies:
- 0.75 kW (1 hp): ~85 %
- 7.5 kW (10 hp): ~91.7 %
- 37 kW (50 hp): ~93.6 %
- 110 kW (150 hp): ~95.4 %
- 375 kW (500 hp): ~96.2 %

The difference between IE2 and IE3 on a motor that runs 8,000 hours a year at electricity rates of £0.15/kWh adds up to hundreds of pounds per year on a large motor.

## How to read a motor nameplate

A motor nameplate carries everything needed to find the shaft power, the electrical input, and the supply current. Key fields:

- **kW or HP**: shaft output power
- **RPM**: synchronous or full-load speed
- **Hz**: supply frequency
- **V**: rated voltage (may list a range, e.g. 380–420 V)
- **A**: full-load current at rated voltage
- **cosφ or PF**: power factor at full load
- **η or eff**: efficiency at full load
- **IP**: ingress protection rating
- **Insulation class**: temperature class of the winding (B, F, H)
- **Duty cycle**: S1 for continuous, S2 for short-time, S3 for intermittent

The electrical input kW is nameplate kW divided by nameplate efficiency. The electrical apparent power (kVA) is the electrical kW divided by the power factor.

## Sizing a generator or transformer for a motor load

A generator or transformer is rated in kVA, not in motor shaft kW. To find the minimum kVA rating for a motor:

1. Convert shaft HP to kW: kW = HP × 0.7457
2. Divide by the motor efficiency to get electrical input kW: input kW = shaft kW / η
3. Divide by the power factor to get kVA: kVA = input kW / PF
4. Apply a service factor (typically 1.25) for starting and other loads

A 50 hp motor with η = 0.93 and PF = 0.88 draws 50 × 0.7457 / 0.93 = 40.1 kW and 40.1 / 0.88 = 45.5 kVA at full load. For a dedicated transformer, the next standard size up is used. For a generator that must also start the motor, the starting kVA, which can be six to ten times the running kVA, governs the sizing. The <a href="/generator-size-calculator/">generator size calculator</a> handles motor starting surge.

## kW versus HP in specifications

Specifications and datasheets from different regions use different units, and mixing them is a common source of error:

- A European pump curve in kW and a North American motor nameplate in HP need the conversion before the motor is confirmed adequate.
- NEC motor circuit sizing uses HP and the full-load current tables in Article 430, not a calculated current. The <a href="/motor-circuit-calculator/">motor circuit calculator</a> does this lookup.
- An IEC motor at 400 V running on a 480 V supply will overspeed and possibly fail. Voltage and frequency must both match the nameplate.
