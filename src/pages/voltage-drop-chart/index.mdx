Voltage drop is the voltage lost along a wire because of its resistance. These charts give it for common AWG sizes in feet and amps, so you can check a run without a calculator. Values are for a two-wire circuit, with the length of the return conductor counted, and for conductors at 75 °C.

## How the numbers are calculated

<div class="formula">V<sub>drop</sub> = 2 × I × L × ρ / A</div>

I is the current, L the one-way length, A the conductor area and ρ the resistivity at the conductor temperature (0.017241 Ω·mm²/m for copper and 0.028264 for aluminum at 20 °C, raised for 75 °C). Reactance is ignored, which is accurate for short single-phase runs in small wire and understates the drop in large conductors. The maximum lengths are the length where the drop reaches 3 % of the supply voltage.

## Worked example

A 20 A load sits 150 ft from the panel on a 240 V circuit in 10 AWG copper. The drop is 0.243 × 20 × 1.5 = 7.3 V, or 3.0 % of 240 V, so the run is at the limit. The chart agrees: the longest 10 AWG run at 20 A is 148 ft.

The same 20 A load, 150 ft away on a 120 V circuit, drops the same 7.3 V, but that is 6.1 % of 120 V. Moving to 6 AWG cuts it to 0.096 × 20 × 1.5 = 2.9 V, or 2.4 %. A higher supply voltage or a larger conductor both fix the problem.

## What the NEC says

The NEC does not set a mandatory voltage drop limit for ordinary circuits. It recommends in informational notes a maximum of 3 % for a branch circuit and 5 % for feeder and branch together. The details are in the <a href="/nec-voltage-drop-guide/">NEC voltage drop guide</a>.

## What the chart leaves out

- **Ampacity.** A wire that satisfies the voltage drop may still be too small for the breaker, and the reverse. Check the ampacity separately. The 14 AWG row is shown for length only; small conductors have their own overcurrent limits.
- **Three-phase.** Multiply the single-phase drop by 0.866 for a balanced three-phase circuit at the same current.
- **Resistance method.** The <a href="/nec-voltage-drop-guide/">guide</a> uses the circular-mil constant K = 12.9, which is about 2 % higher than these exact values. Both are good estimates.

## Questions

### What size wire for 100 ft at 20 amps?
At 240 V, 12 AWG gives 0.386 × 20 × 1 = 7.7 V which is 3.2 %, so 10 AWG is the next choice with 4.9 V (2.0 %). At 120 V the same run needs 8 AWG for under 3 %.

### Is aluminum drop higher than copper?
About 65 % higher at the same size, because aluminum resistivity is higher. A larger aluminum size gives the same drop as copper.

### Does the drop change with temperature?
Yes. A hot conductor has higher resistance. The chart uses 75 °C. At 20 °C the drop is about 18 % lower.
