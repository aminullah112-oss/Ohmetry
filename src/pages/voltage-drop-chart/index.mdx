Voltage drop is the voltage lost along a wire because of its resistance. These charts give it for common AWG sizes in feet and amps, so you can check a run without a calculator. Values are for a two-wire circuit, with the length of the return conductor counted, and for conductors at 75 °C.

## How the numbers are calculated

<div class="formula">V<sub>drop</sub> = 2 × I × L × ρ / A</div>

I is the current, L the one-way length, A the conductor area and ρ the resistivity at the conductor temperature (0.017241 Ω·mm²/m for copper and 0.028264 for aluminum at 20 °C, raised for 75 °C). Reactance is ignored, which is accurate for short single-phase runs in small wire and understates the drop in large conductors. The maximum lengths are the length where the drop reaches 3 % of the supply voltage.

## Worked example

A 20 A load sits 150 ft from the panel on a 240 V circuit in 10 AWG copper. The drop is 0.243 × 20 × 1.5 = 7.3 V, or 3.0 % of 240 V, so the run is at the limit. The chart agrees: the longest 10 AWG run at 20 A is 148 ft.

The same 20 A load, 150 ft away on a 120 V circuit, drops the same 7.3 V, but that is 6.1 % of 120 V. Moving to 6 AWG cuts it to 0.096 × 20 × 1.5 = 2.9 V, or 2.4 %. A higher supply voltage or a larger conductor both fix the problem.

## What the NEC says

The NEC does not set a mandatory voltage drop limit for ordinary circuits. It recommends in informational notes a maximum of 3 % for a branch circuit and 5 % for feeder and branch together. The details are in the <a href="/nec-voltage-drop-guide/">NEC voltage drop guide</a>.

## What the chart leaves out

- **Ampacity.** A wire that satisfies the voltage drop may still be too small for the breaker, and the reverse. Check the ampacity separately. The 14 AWG row is shown for length only; small conductors have their own overcurrent limits.
- **Three-phase.** Multiply the single-phase drop by 0.866 for a balanced three-phase circuit at the same current.
- **Resistance method.** The <a href="/nec-voltage-drop-guide/">guide</a> uses the circular-mil constant K = 12.9, which is about 2 % higher than these exact values. Both are good estimates.

## Questions

### What size wire for 100 ft at 20 amps?
At 240 V, 12 AWG gives 0.386 × 20 × 1 = 7.7 V which is 3.2 %, so 10 AWG is the next choice with 4.9 V (2.0 %). At 120 V the same run needs 8 AWG for under 3 %.

### Is aluminum drop higher than copper?
About 65 % higher at the same size, because aluminum resistivity is higher. A larger aluminum size gives the same drop as copper.

### Does the drop change with temperature?
Yes. A hot conductor has higher resistance. The chart uses 75 °C. At 20 °C the drop is about 18 % lower.

## Voltage drop limits by application

| Application | Recommended limit | Standard |
|---|---|---|
| NEC branch circuit | 3 % | NEC informational note to 210.19(A) |
| NEC feeder + branch combined | 5 % | NEC informational note to 215.2(A) |
| NEC sensitive electronic equipment | 2.5 % | NEC 647.4 |
| IEC 60364 (residential, lighting) | 3 % | IEC 60364-5-52 |
| IEC 60364 (industrial, motor) | 5 % | IEC 60364-5-52 |
| Solar PV DC wiring | 1–2 % | NEC 690.8 guidance |
| Battery-to-inverter | 0.5–1 % | Manufacturer requirement |

A motor that starts under voltage draw 50–60 % more starting current, so voltage drop matters more for motor circuits than for heaters. At 95 % of rated voltage a motor still starts; at 90 % it may not.

## Worked example: subpanel feeder

A workshop subpanel sits 200 ft from the main panel and is fed at 240 V with a 60 A continuous load.

**Allowed drop:** 3 % of 240 V = 7.2 V. Combined feeder + branch limit is 5 %, so the feeder gets 2–3 %.

**Formula:** Area (kcmil) = 2 × L × I × K / (V × %drop / 100), where K = 12.9 for copper at 75 °C.
Area = 2 × 200 × 60 × 12.9 / (240 × 3) = 86 kcmil.

**Closest AWG:** 2 AWG copper (66.4 kcmil) drops 4.0 %. 1 AWG (83.7 kcmil) drops 3.2 %. 1/0 AWG (105.6 kcmil) drops 2.5 %. For the 3 % limit, **1 AWG** is the minimum.

**Check against ampacity:** 1 AWG THHN at 75 °C = 130 A, well above 60 A continuous × 1.25 = 75 A. The drop governs, not the ampacity.

## Copper vs aluminum voltage drop

Aluminum has 61 % of copper's conductivity, so the same wire size drops 1.64× more. To match copper's drop, choose aluminum two AWG sizes larger:

| Copper | Aluminum equivalent | Voltage drop match |
|---|---|---|
| 12 AWG | 10 AWG Al | ±2 % |
| 10 AWG | 8 AWG Al | ±2 % |
| 8 AWG | 6 AWG Al | ±3 % |
| 6 AWG | 4 AWG Al | ±2 % |
| 4 AWG | 2 AWG Al | ±2 % |
| 2 AWG | 1/0 AWG Al | ±2 % |

Aluminum feeders of 100 A and above are common and cost-effective where the extra two AWG sizes fit the conduit. Aluminum is not permitted for 15 and 20 A branch circuits under NEC 310.106(B).

## EV charger and motor circuit drop considerations

**EV charger (Level 2, 7.6 kW, 240 V, 32 A):** At 100 ft on 10 AWG copper, drop = 0.243 × 32 × 1 = 7.8 V = 3.2 % of 240 V. Use 8 AWG for 100 ft or longer runs (4.9 V = 2.0 %).

**Heat pump motor (5 hp, 240 V, 28 A, NEC table current):** At 150 ft on 10 AWG, drop = 0.243 × 28 × 1.5 = 10.2 V = 4.3 % — over the 3 % limit. Use 8 AWG: 0.096 × 28 × 1.5 = 4.0 V = 1.7 %.

## More Q&As

### Does the 3 % limit apply to 240 V and 120 V circuits the same way?
The percentage is the same, but the allowable volts differ: 3 % of 240 V = 7.2 V, while 3 % of 120 V = 3.6 V. A 120 V circuit has half the voltage to spend, so for the same current and wire size it reaches the limit at half the distance.

### How do I apply the chart to a three-phase circuit?
Multiply the two-wire drop by 0.866 for a balanced three-phase circuit. 10 AWG at 20 A per phase over 100 ft drops 0.243 × 20 × 1 × 0.866 = 4.2 V on a 240 V three-phase system (1.75 %).

### Does voltage drop affect energy bills?
Yes, but the effect is small on a typical branch circuit. A 3 % drop means 3 % of the circuit's power is dissipated in the wire as heat rather than delivered to the load. On a 2,000 W circuit running 8 h/day, that is 60 W × 8 h = 480 Wh/day wasted. At $0.15/kWh that is $26/year — usually less than the cost of the larger wire.
